\point{Editor E4: relation to deformation-based approaches}{Better establish the contribution relative to existing deformation-based approaches.}
\begin{reply}
WarpVox enables deformation-based correction of an already fused TSDF without replaying historical volumetric integration. Its target construction exploits the invariance of sensor-frame range measurements to a world-frame pose correction: cached measurements are re-associated with the current surface under the old poses, then transformed by the revised poses to provide surface targets. Its source-supported reconstruction combines the warped zero set with the source volume's observed support, integration weights, sign information, and free space, returning a native TSDF for continued fusion. Under the same input mesh and pre-/post-correction trajectories, WarpVox improves F@25/F@50 over Kimera-PGMO by 0.26/0.91 percentage points and reduces surface-correction core time from 8.231 to 3.515\,s. The repeated-cycle experiment demonstrates continued fusion from the corrected volume; the additional ElasticFusion experiments compare complete systems on TUM and Kimera-Multi. R2-S2 and R2-S3 explain the mechanisms and the corresponding evidence.
\end{reply}

\point{R1-2: Stage-3 pseudocode}{The four-stage structure is easy to follow, and the introduction clearly motivates the problem. Section III-D is harder to follow because several filtering, target-adjustment, and mesh-repair operations are described in succession. Pseudocode showing their order would help.}
\begin{reply}
Algorithm 1 gives the Stage-3 operations in execution order: label connected components; remove degenerate faces and control-free fragments; reject outlier controls; assign synthetic controls; construct and constrain the proxy when needed; solve ARAP; transfer displacements; and repair or remove invalid faces. Each step refers to its definition in Section III-D. A repair pass stops when it reduces neither the number of invalid faces nor the number of aspect-ratio violations, with at most three passes and 80 Jacobi steps per pass. This ordering makes the filtering, target consolidation, deformation, and repair dependencies explicit.

For completeness, the execution sequence is reproduced here. The input is the source triangle mesh and its measured control displacements; the output is a source/warped surface pair with shared face indices.
\begin{enumerate}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
\item Label mesh components. Remove degenerate faces and components with fewer than 100 faces that contain no measured control.
\item Predict each measured control displacement from neighbouring controls. Apply the median--MAD rejection once. Remove small components that lose all controls; give every other unconstrained component three synthetic controls.
\item For more than 78,000 source vertices, build the component-aware QEM proxy, average the control displacements within each proxy cluster, and consolidate the proxy targets using the Welsch rule. Use the direct source-mesh path when the proxy fails the checks listed in R2-D15.
\item Solve ARAP for 20 local--global iterations with prescribed control positions. On the proxy path, transfer the resulting displacements to source vertices through incident proxy triangles.
\item Test edge stretch, area growth, and aspect ratio. Relax the displacements of vertices on flagged faces, re-test, and stop when the faces satisfy the criteria or the repair makes no progress, subject to the iteration limits above.
\item Remove faces that still violate the edge or area bound from both surfaces. Preserve the shared face indices for Stage 4.
\end{enumerate}
\end{reply}

For observation $r$ of vertex $j$, the aggregation weight is
\[

\alpha_{rj}/(1+z^m_{rj}/\zeta_m), & \text{RGB-D},\\

With fewer than three valid samples, all are retained. R2-D3--D4 and R2-D11 specify the neighbourhood and robust rejection rules; R2-D18 gives the repair tests.

\point{R2-S2: Conceptual distinction from ElasticFusion}{The difference seems to be the use of triangles in place of surfels for computing the deformations.}
\begin{reply}
WarpVox corrects an externally maintained TSDF after a back-end trajectory revision. Its targets exploit sensor-frame measurement invariance: projective reassociation identifies cached measurements consistent with the current surface, and the corrected poses place those measurements at their revised world positions. Its reconstruction combines distances from the warped surface with source-derived support, sign, integration weights, and free space, preserving the volumetric state needed to resume TSDF fusion. ElasticFusion derives deformation constraints through local and global surface registration within its surfel SLAM system; Kimera-PGMO propagates trajectory corrections through a deformation graph to a timestamped mesh. WarpVox contributes measurement-derived surface targets and source-supported volumetric reconstruction, with ARAP and QEM providing the deformation machinery. On the same input mesh and pre-/post-correction trajectories, WarpVox improves F@25/F@50 by 0.26/0.91 percentage points over Kimera-PGMO at $2.34\times$ lower surface-correction core time. Stage 4 then reconstructs a native TSDF in another 1.277\,s, for 4.792\,s in total. The repeated-cycle experiment demonstrates that the resulting volume supports subsequent fusion and correction.
\end{reply}

\subsection*{Sec. III-A}

\point{R2-A1}{How are TSDF and weight field represented? Regular 3D grid?}
\begin{reply}
Voxblox uses a regular grid of voxel size $s$, allocated sparsely in blocks of $16^3$ voxels. Each voxel centre $x$ stores a truncated signed distance $\Phi^-(x)\in[-\tau,\tau]$ and an integration weight $w^-(x)\ge0$. Unallocated voxels have zero weight. Section III-A defines both fields and the grid representation before introducing the correction.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-A2}{V\textasciicircum{}- and F are not defined.}
\begin{reply}
Sec.~III-A defines $\mathcal M^-=(V^-,F)$ as an indexed triangle mesh with vertex positions $V^-$ and triangles $F$ stored as vertex-index triples.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-A3}{What do "active TSDF cells" mean? Cells around the zero-level set?}
\begin{reply}
Active cells are the Marching-Cubes cells that emit triangles around the zero level set. Section III-F defines the term where it enters the cost analysis.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-A4}{What does "modality-native" mean?}
\begin{reply}
The term is removed. Each keyframe measurement is kept in its own sensor frame, as a depth image (RGB-D) or a spherical range image (LiDAR), and therefore stays valid after any pose correction (Sec.~III-A).
\end{reply}

\subsection*{Sec. III-B}

\point{R2-B1}{What kind of spatial indexing scheme allows storing/retrieving visibility relationship between vertices and sensors?}
\begin{reply}
Candidate keyframes are retrieved by spatial proximity of their sensor centres under the pre-correction poses: a $k$-d tree with a 14.7\,m radius for RGB-D, and a grid with an 80\,m search radius for LiDAR. The index is rebuilt at each correction. Projecting a vertex into each candidate range image and applying the gate in Eq.~(2) establishes measurement consistency. In particular, an occluder's return is rejected when its discrepancy from the vertex exceeds the gate tolerance. The index stores sensor positions; the projective test determines which measurements support a vertex.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-B2}{What does it mean to "sample the projected depth"? Do you mean get the closest?}
\begin{reply}
For RGB-D, the depth is bilinearly interpolated from the four pixels around the projected position, with at least two valid pixels, and back-projected along the same ray. For LiDAR, the stored return closest in 3-D to the projected source vertex is selected within a $3\times3$ pixel window. Section III-B defines the sampling operator and its 3-D output.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-B3}{What is the exact formula for association quality, \textbackslash{}alpha\_\{rj\}?}
\begin{reply}
Section III-B now defines the association reliability as
\[
\alpha_{rj}=\frac{|\cos\phi_{rj}|}{1+z^-_{rj}/z_0}
\exp(-\delta_{rj}/\sigma_g)\exp(-|z^m_{rj}-z^-_{rj}|/\sigma_z).
\]
Here $\delta_{rj}=\|m_{rj}-\hat v_{rj}^-\|$ is the distance between the sampled point and the projected source vertex in the sensor frame. The quantities $z^-_{rj}$ and $z^m_{rj}$ are their depths (RGB-D) or ranges (LiDAR), and $\phi_{rj}$ is the angle between the source normal and the viewing ray. The factors encode incidence angle, range, and measurement discrepancy; the last exponential applies to RGB-D only. The gate is $\delta_{rj}\leq g_0+g_1z^-_{rj}$; LiDAR also requires $|z^m_{rj}-z^-_{rj}|$ to meet the same bound. R1-3(c) lists the modality-specific constants and the subsequent target weights $\omega_{rj}$.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-B4}{What are the criteria for retaining a candidate? Can multiple candidates be retained? What if none are retained?}
\begin{reply}
A candidate is retained when a valid sample passes the geometric gate and, for RGB-D, satisfies $\alpha_{rj}\ge\alpha_{\min}$. Up to $n_{\max}=15$ of the most reliable candidates are retained per vertex. LiDAR collects at most 30 candidates in grid-cell and then trajectory order before selecting the retained set. A vertex with no retained observation follows the Stage-3 deformation without a measured target (Sec.~III-B).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-B5}{Is m\_\{rj\} a point or an image?}
\begin{reply}
$m_{rj}\in\mathbb R^3$ is one sampled 3-D point in the sensor frame of keyframe $r$. The range image is $D_r$; sampling it at the projection of source vertex $j$ produces $m_{rj}$. Equation (1) now distinguishes the image, projected coordinates, and returned point.
\end{reply}

\subsection*{Sec. III-C}

\point{R2-C1}{What do RGB-D/LIDAR "records" mean? Are these just frames? Also, why are RGB-D associated with timestamp but LIDAR with poses?}
\begin{reply}
They are observations, each from one keyframe (an RGB-D depth image or a LiDAR scan). Both modalities use the corrected pose of that keyframe: a LiDAR scan has its own trajectory entry, while an RGB-D keyframe time is read from $T^+$ or interpolated between the bracketing poses when they are at most three median pose intervals apart (Sec.~III-C, Table~I).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-C2}{What if there are no candidates r satisfying Eq. 5?}
\begin{reply}
For a nonempty set of observations with valid corrected poses, the trimming threshold is at least the median residual because $\kappa>1$. At least half of those observations survive; with fewer than three, all are retained. If no observation has a valid corrected pose, $\mathcal J_j=\emptyset$ and $v_j^*=v_j^-$, yielding a zero-displacement measured control. A vertex with no retained observation in Stage 1 follows the surface deformation without a measured target. Sections III-B and III-C distinguish these two cases.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-C3}{What does "supported source vertex" mean?}
\begin{reply}
It is now called an observed vertex, i.e., a vertex with at least one observation ($\mathcal O_j\neq\emptyset$); it yields one target $v_j^*$ and becomes a measured control (Secs.~III-B to III-D).
\end{reply}

\subsection*{Sec. III-D}

\point{R2-D1}{What do "controls" mean?}
\begin{reply}
A control is a vertex with a prescribed deformed position. Each observed source vertex becomes a measured control with target $v_j^*$ and displacement $d_j=v_j^*-v_j^-$. Components without a surviving measured control receive the synthetic controls described in R2-D7. The ARAP solve fixes controlled positions and solves for the remaining vertex positions; subsequent repair is described separately in R2-D18.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D2}{What do "connected components" mean? Are these connected components of the mesh? How are they tracked?}
\begin{reply}
These are connected components of the triangle mesh: vertices connected through mesh edges belong to the same component. Components are recomputed for the current source mesh at each correction. Stage 3 retains no component-tracking state across correction events. Section III-D defines mesh connectivity before introducing component-wise processing.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D3}{What does "component-restricted inverse-distance predictor" do? I.e. what significance does connectivity have in predicting inverse distance?}
\begin{reply}
The predictor estimates a control displacement as the inverse-squared-distance weighted mean of neighbouring control displacements (Eq.~(8)). Among the $k=20$ nearest controls, it uses those on the same connected component when at least $n_{\min}=16$ are available; otherwise it uses all $k$. Component identity helps distinguish nearby copies of a surface produced by drift, which can require different corrections despite their spatial proximity.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D4}{What is a "median - MAD residual test"? Residual between what? What is MAD?}
\begin{reply}
The test compares a measured control displacement $d_j$ with the neighbourhood prediction $\hat d_j$. Over all tested controls, define
\[
e_j=\|d_j-\hat d_j\|,\qquad
\mu=\operatorname{median}_j e_j,\qquad
\mathrm{MAD}=\operatorname{median}_j|e_j-\mu|.
\]
A control is rejected when
\[
e_j>\mu+1.5\max(1.4826\,\mathrm{MAD},0.05\,\mathrm{m}).
\]
The factor 1.4826 converts MAD into a Gaussian-consistent scale estimate; the 0.05\,m floor prevents a nearly constant neighbourhood from producing a vanishing threshold. This rejection is applied once, before synthetic controls and proxy construction, as specified in Eq.~(9) and Algorithm 1.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D5}{What does "statistically isolated control" mean? Do you mean outliers?}
\begin{reply}
These are outlier controls. We replaced the term with ``outlier'' and give the test explicitly in Eq.~(9): a control is rejected when its displacement-prediction residual exceeds the median residual plus the scaled MAD threshold.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D6}{What is the difference between "omitted components" (due to being "supported only by rejected controls") and "Retained components without a surviving measured control"? I.e., how can components be retained without surviving "controls" if they are omitted?}
\begin{reply}
Omitted components are fragments (fewer than $N_{\rm f}$ faces) that were kept only because they carried controls and lost all of them in step (a). Every other component without a surviving control, including one that never had a control, is retained and receives synthetic controls (step (b)).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D7}{Constraints were not yet defined here, yet there are descriptions of "receiving robust motion constraints"}
\begin{reply}
Controls are now defined at the start of Sec.~III-D, before use. For each retained component without a surviving measured control, step (b) places synthetic controls at the lowest-index vertex, the vertex farthest from it, and the vertex farthest from both. Their common displacement is the coordinate-wise median displacement of the 20 surviving controls nearest to the component centroid. These prescribed positions supply the rigid motion that the component's ARAP energy leaves undetermined.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D8}{What does "QEM" stand for?}
\begin{reply}
Quadric error metric (Garland and Heckbert, 1997); step (c) expands the acronym and describes the proxy construction. Each cluster is represented by the point of lowest quadric error among the cell-clamped QEM minimizer, the cluster centroid and its first vertex.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D9}{Eq. 7: is this the "control" or the corrected proxy vertex position?}
\begin{reply}
The submitted Eq.~(7) defines an initial proxy target, denoted $p_a^{(0)}$ in the revision: the proxy rest position plus the average displacement of its clustered controls. The Welsch consolidation produces the constrained target $p_a^*$, and the ARAP solve produces the deformed position $p_a^+$. These three quantities are distinguished in Eqs.~(10)--(12).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D10}{What does "controlled proxy vertex" mean? How are vertices controlled/uncontrolled?}
\begin{reply}
A proxy vertex is controlled if at least one control maps to it ($\mathcal K_a\neq\emptyset$); a proxy component that receives no control, e.g., one split off when degenerate proxy triangles are dropped, is held at three of its vertices, which step (d) revises like synthetic targets. Only uncontrolled vertices are unknowns of Eq.~(12).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D11}{What does "only when it is a gross residual" mean? What does it mean for residuals to be a strict majority? What are the "synthetic targets"?}
\begin{reply}
For controlled proxy vertex $a$, a local rigid fit predicts $\hat p_a=\hat R_ap_a^-+\hat t_a$ from neighbouring measured proxy targets. The residual is $r_a=\|p_a^{(0)}-\hat p_a\|$. A measured target is classified as gross when $r_a\ge4.685\sigma_a$, where $\sigma_a$ is the median fitting residual plus 1.4826 times its MAD.

Let $N_K$ be the number of fitted measured targets in component $K$, and $G_K$ the number classified as gross. A strict majority means $2G_K>N_K$. Equation (11) then gives
\[
p_a^*=\begin{cases}
\hat p_a, & a\text{ is gross and }2G_K>N_K,\\
\hat p_a+\nu(r_a)(p_a^{(0)}-\hat p_a), & a\text{ has a synthetic target},\\
p_a^{(0)}, & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\qquad \nu(r)=\exp[-r^2/(2\sigma_a^2)].
\]
The fit uses up to 20 nearest measured proxy controls, excluding $a$, with at least six required for fitting. It performs four rounds of Welsch reweighting multiplied by a fixed prior favouring near, distance-preserving, and well-observed neighbours. A target with too few fitting neighbours keeps its initial value. Thus, the majority test concerns measured targets within a component; synthetic targets follow the separate interpolation rule above.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D12}{Eq. 8: p\_\{a\}\textasciicircum{}\{+\} and p\_b\textasciicircum{}\{+\} are not defined. Should these be p\_\{a\}\textasciicircum{}\{\textbackslash{}star\}? What is P\textasciicircum{}+? Why is it an optimization variable?}
\begin{reply}
$P^+=\{p_a^+\}$ are the deformed proxy positions: controlled ones are fixed to $p_a^*$, and the others are the unknowns of Eq.~(12), now written in the spokes-and-rims form that libigl solves (step (e)).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D13}{What does "proxy one-ring" mean?}
\begin{reply}
$\operatorname{star}_P(a)$ is the one-ring of proxy triangles incident to vertex $a$ (Sec.~III-D (e)).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D14}{Give the formula for "symmetric cotangent weights"}
\begin{reply}
$c^f_{bc}=\frac12\cot\vartheta^f_{bc}$, with $\vartheta^f_{bc}$ the angle opposite edge $(b,c)$ in rest triangle $f$; summed over the two triangles of an edge, this is the symmetric cotangent weight (Sec.~III-D (e)). The factor $1/3$ in Eq.~(12) offsets that each triangle is visited from its three corners.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D15}{Is the proposed method using the "proxy" or the source mesh?}
\begin{reply}
Meshes with more than $1.3P_{\max}$ vertices use a coarse proxy for the ARAP solve, with $P_{\max}=60{,}000$. Its displacements are then transferred to the source mesh, which supplies the output surface. Smaller meshes are solved directly. The implementation also falls back to the source mesh when proxy construction yields fewer than 100 vertices or faces, fewer than 10\% or more than 99\% controlled vertices, or failed ARAP precomputation. Section III-D and Algorithm 1 describe the two paths; the conditions above specify when the direct source-mesh path is used.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D16}{What does "topology-local" mean?}
\begin{reply}
The interpolating triangle is selected from the proxy triangles incident to the vertex's own proxy vertex $\pi(j)$. Mesh connectivity defines this search neighbourhood (Sec.~III-D (f), Eq.~(13)).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D17}{What does "star(\textbackslash{}pi(j))" mean?}
\begin{reply}
$\operatorname{star}_P(\pi(j))$ is the set of proxy triangles incident to $\pi(j)$; since clusters never span two components, these triangles lie on the vertex's own component, and the transfer passes no motion between disconnected surfaces (Sec.~III-D (c), (f)). A vertex whose proxy vertex has no incident triangle takes the mean control displacement of its component.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D18}{How are "edge-stretch, area-change or aspect-ratio bounds" defined mathematically?}
\begin{reply}
For corresponding source and warped triangle edge lengths $\ell_e^-$ and $\ell_e^+$, areas $A^-$ and $A^+$, and longest warped edge $\ell^+_{\max}$, the measures are
\[
\xi^{\rm e}=\max_e\frac{\ell_e^+}{\ell_e^-},\qquad
\xi^{\rm A}=\frac{A^+}{A^-},\qquad
\xi^{\rm asp}=\frac{(\ell^+_{\max})^2}{2A^+}.
\]
A face is flagged when $\xi^{\rm e}\ge3$, $\xi^{\rm A}\ge6$, or both $\xi^{\rm asp}\ge20$ and $\ell^+_{\max}\ge0.5$\,m. Jacobi steps relax the displacements of its vertices, including controlled vertices, toward their neighbour means while holding other vertices fixed. Faces are re-tested after each pass. Repair uses at most three passes with at most 80 steps each and stops when neither the invalid-face count nor the aspect-violation count decreases. Faces still violating the edge or area bound are removed from both paired surfaces.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D19}{What does "observed support" mean?}
\begin{reply}
Observed source support is $\Omega^-=\{x:w^-(x)>0\}$. It contains voxels carrying integration evidence, including both the surface neighbourhood and observed free space. Section III-A separates this volumetric set from observed mesh vertices, which are defined by retained keyframe correspondences in Section III-B.
\end{reply}

\subsection*{Sec. III-E}

\point{R2-E1}{What is "surface-local reconstruction/domain"? Is there a "surface-global reconstruction/domain"?}
\begin{reply}
Surface-local reconstruction processes voxel centres within truncation distance $\tau$ of the warped surface, restricted to blocks containing a warped vertex and their 26 neighbours. The second part of Stage 4 transports supported source free space. These two domains define the reconstruction in Section III-E.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E2}{The first paragraph says the "sign orientation" comes from the source mesh, whereas Eq. 12 claims it is "determined separately from the source field".}
\begin{reply}
The warped surface supplies distance magnitudes and geometric normals. The source TSDF guides the transferred sign through the central difference across the paired source face; when that quantity is unavailable, the rule uses a sign-consistent interpolated source sample, then the warped-face sign as a final fallback. The revised Stage-4 opening and Eq.~(19) specify these sources and their priority.

Specifically, for corresponding closest points $q^-$ and $q^+$ on the source and warped faces, define
\[
g_f=\frac{\bar\Phi^-(q^-+s n_f^-)-\bar\Phi^-(q^--s n_f^-)}{2s},
\qquad s_f^+(x)=\operatorname{sgn}((x-q^+)^\top n_f^+).
\]
The source-field orientation is available when the source sample at the pulled-back voxel and both probes are valid and $g_f\ne0$. The transferred sign is then $s_f^+(x)\operatorname{sgn}(g_f)$. Otherwise, the sign-consistent interpolated source value is used when available; the final fallback is $s_f^+(x)$. Thus, the mesh supplies the oriented geometric frame, while the source field checks its free-space orientation.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E3}{What does "weight provides support" mean?}
\begin{reply}
A positive source weight records observed support. When the trilinear source sample is valid, Eq.~(18) transfers its interpolated weight. Otherwise, it takes the weight of the containing source voxel. In both cases, the corrected distance magnitude is computed from the warped surface. The source weight and reconstructed distance therefore carry distinct information in the corrected voxel.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E4}{What is the fallback branch?}
\begin{reply}
The fallback handles voxels with no transferred source weight within $\rho=1.5s$ of the warped surface. With $r(x)$ the distance to that surface, the weight is
\[
w_{\rm fb}(x)=\min\{1,\max\{0,(\rho-r(x))/s\}\}.
\]
The distance magnitude comes from the warped mesh and its sign from the oriented warped face. This weight is a geometric prior for continued fusion, not an observation count. The prior is withheld at open boundaries, as detailed in R2-E8. Equation (20) and the fallback paragraph state these conditions explicitly.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E5}{What is a "consistent source sample"?}
\begin{reply}
A trilinear source sample is valid when every voxel with a nonzero interpolation coefficient has positive integration weight. It is sign-consistent when those voxels also share the same nonzero distance sign. This condition concerns the contributing voxels, so cancellation between positive and negative values in the interpolated result does not establish sign consistency. Section III-E defines the two tests before Eq.~(18).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E6}{What is the difference between w\_\{tr\}\textasciicircum{}+ and w\_\{src\}\textasciicircum{}+?}
\begin{reply}
The earlier $w_{\rm tr}^+$ duplicated $w_{\rm src}^+$ on the transferred-voxel set. We removed the redundant symbol and the former Eq.~(13). Equation (18) defines the source weight once, and Eq.~(22) uses it directly on $\Omega_{\rm tr}$.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E7}{How is n\_f\textasciicircum{}+ defined?}
\begin{reply}
$n_f^+$ is the unit normal of warped face $f=(a,b,c)$, oriented by the vertex order of $F$; Sec.~III-E also defines the paired frames built from it (Eq.~(16)).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E8}{How does the algorithm determine if the closest surface feature is an open boundary?}
\begin{reply}
Boundary edges are edges of exactly one face. The prior is withheld when the closest point lies on such an edge or its end vertex, because there the closest-point direction is not normal to the surface (Sec.~III-E, fallback paragraph).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E9}{What does "supported voxel" mean?}
\begin{reply}
A voxel with positive weight, $w>0$ (Sec.~III-A); $\Omega^+$ is the set of supported output voxels (Eq.~(22)).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E10}{What is a "fallback support"? What are "narrow samples"?}
\begin{reply}
Fallback support is $\Omega_{\rm fb}=\{x:w_{\rm fb}(x)>0\}$. The former phrase ``narrow samples'' refers to the narrow set $\mathcal N_{\rm tr}$ of voxels with positive transferred source weight and distance $r(x)\le\rho$ from the warped surface. The extended set $\mathcal E$ covers $\rho<r(x)\le\tau$: a voxel with a valid trilinear source sample is retained there only when the source-field central difference $g_f$ fixes its sign; a weight-only voxel uses the warped-face sign.

After removing sign conflicts, a face-connected flood fill from $\mathcal N_{\rm tr}\cup\Omega_{\rm fb}$ removes disconnected extended voxels. The retained narrow and extended voxels form $\Omega_{\rm tr}$. Transported free space is inserted last into zero-weight output voxels, with insertions next to retained negative voxels rejected. Figure~\ref{fig:reply-mechanisms}(c) and Section III-E distinguish these sets and their roles.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E11}{What makes a source face valid or invalid?}
\begin{reply}
A group takes the closest source face within the face search radius $16s$ of its mean voxel centre; it is discarded if there is none or if that face or its warped copy is degenerate, so that a frame cannot be built (Sec.~III-E, Eq.~(21)).
\end{reply}

\subsection*{Sec. III-F}

\point{R2-F1}{What are C\_\{def\} and C\_\{vol\}?}
\begin{reply}
$C_{\rm def}$ is the Stage-3 cost of deformation, including proxy construction, target consolidation, ARAP, and transfer or repair. $C_{\rm vol}$ is the Stage-4 cost of TSDF reconstruction and free-space transport. Section III-F writes
\[
C_{\rm def}=O(A\log A)+C_{\rm P}(P),\qquad
C_{\rm vol}=O((A+X^-+X^+)\log A),
\]
where $A$ counts source Marching-Cubes cells emitting triangles, $P$ is the proxy size, and $X^-,X^+$ count source and output voxel slots. $C_{\rm P}$ covers the proxy-level Welsch fitting and ARAP solve. The volume term includes construction and use of the closest-triangle search structures.
\end{reply}

\subsection*{Sec. IV-A}

\point{R2-IVA1}{Fully define F@10/25/50. Are these F1-score at 10/25/50cm thresholds?}
\begin{reply}
F@10/25/50 denotes the F1-score at Euclidean distance thresholds of 10, 25, and 50\,cm. Let $\mathcal P$ and $\mathcal G$ be the predicted and reference point sets and $d(p,\mathcal X)=\min_{x\in\mathcal X}\|p-x\|$. We use
\[
P_\eta=\frac{|\{p\in\mathcal P:d(p,\mathcal G)<\eta\}|}{|\mathcal P|},\quad
R_\eta=\frac{|\{g\in\mathcal G:d(g,\mathcal P)<\eta\}|}{|\mathcal G|},\quad
F_\eta=100\frac{2P_\eta R_\eta}{P_\eta+R_\eta}.
\]
For a nonempty pair of point sets with $P_\eta+R_\eta=0$, the score is zero. Predicted meshes are sampled uniformly by area with two million points; the reference is the full cropped ground-truth cloud. Wavemap uses its occupancy point cloud, randomly subsampled to at most two million points. This definition, sampling protocol, and percentage convention form the replacement Metrics paragraph in Section IV-A.
\end{reply}

We computed the symmetric, unsquared Chamfer distance for all reconstruction baselines and the PGMO comparison:
\[
\mathrm{CD}=\frac12\left(\frac{1}{|\mathcal P|}\sum_{p\in\mathcal P}d(p,\mathcal G)
+\frac{1}{|\mathcal G|}\sum_{g\in\mathcal G}d(g,\mathcal P)\right).
\]
The table reports the arithmetic mean of the per-event, untruncated distances in centimetres. The two directed means have equal weight, independently of the numbers of predicted and reference points. Distances are evaluated on the same cases as the F-scores.

WarpVox reduces CD by 26.1\,cm relative to one-shot PGMO under shared inputs. The reconstruction table reports WarpVox and reintegration on both datasets, complementing their F-score and update-time comparison with an untruncated distance measure that retains contributions from large reconstruction errors.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-IVA3}{Table II shows inconsistent relative portions between different datasets. Please discuss why some datasets incur larger time in, e.g., association, whereas other datasets incur larger time in target estimation.}
\begin{reply}
The stages depend on different workloads. RGB-D queries every source vertex and transforms up to 15 retained measurements per vertex, with corrected poses interpolated by timestamp where needed. LiDAR queries one vertex per occupied 0.5\,m cell and reads corrected scan poses directly. Its association also searches a $3\times3$ spherical-image window. These differences help explain the RGB-D association/target times of 0.199/0.399\,s and the KITTI values of 0.310/0.069\,s. Deformation and TSDF reconstruction depend on surface and volume size; their KITTI times are 2.457 and 1.888\,s, compared with 1.419 and 0.231\,s on Kimera-Multi at 25\,cm. The 10\,cm Kimera row averages a separate 11-case subset. The timing columns correspond to Stage 1 (Assoc.), Stage 2 (Target), Stage 3 (ARAP and Topol.), and Stage 4 (TSDF).
\end{reply}

\begin{reply}
A ``bag'' is the ROS recording of one robot's Kimera-Multi sequence. The ten recordings are: 10\_14 acl\_jackal2, sparkal1, sparkal2; 12\_07 acl\_jackal2, hathor, sparkal1; and 12\_08 acl\_jackal2, apis, sobek, thoth. The corresponding replacement captions are ``Corrected-surface comparison on the final states of ten Kimera-Multi sequences'' and ``Reconstruction comparison at the last evaluated loop event of each Kimera-Multi sequence.''
\end{reply}
