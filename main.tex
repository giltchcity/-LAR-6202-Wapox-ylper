\section*{Response to the Editor}
We group the Editor's report into four requests.
\point{Editor E1: clarity and reproducibility}{Substantial revisions are needed to improve methodological clarity and reproducibility. The revision should provide missing algorithmic details and parameters.}
\begin{reply}
Section III now defines the requested terms and update rules. Table~I lists the parameters, and Algorithm~1 specifies the order of the Stage-3 steps. Figures~3 -- 5 illustrate association, clustering, and the support of the output TSDF. We use one fixed parameter set per modality (RGB-D or LiDAR) across datasets and sequences. The presentation follows the four stages:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,topsep=2pt,itemsep=2pt]
\item Stage~1 identifies the keyframe measurements that observed each surface vertex;
\item Stage~2 transforms those measurements with the corrected poses and aggregates them into a target;
\item Stage~3 deforms a proxy mesh toward the targets using ARAP;
\item Stage~4 reconstructs a standard TSDF using the corrected surface and the source TSDF's weights, signs, and observed space.
\end{itemize}
The replies to Reviewer~2 identify the corresponding definitions; R1-3(c) reports threshold sensitivity.
\end{reply}
\point{Editor E2: relation to deformation-based approaches}{Better establish the contribution relative to existing deformation-based approaches.}
\begin{reply}
WarpVox differs from existing deformation-based methods in the source of its correction and the representation it produces (R2-S2). ElasticFusion, Kintinuous, and Kimera-PGMO deform surfels, point clouds, or meshes. WarpVox takes the corrected trajectory from an external SLAM back-end. It corrects a fused TSDF without full reintegration and returns a standard TSDF that supports continued fusion. To our knowledge, it is the first deformation-based method to return such a TSDF (Sections I -- II).

In the submitted manuscript, we compared WarpVox with Kimera-PGMO (Section IV-C). Kimera-PGMO corrects the trajectory and the mesh together with an embedded deformation graph. With the same input mesh and trajectories, WarpVox achieves higher F@25/F@50 and a lower Chamfer distance while taking less than half the surface-correction time. The full WarpVox update, including TSDF reconstruction, also takes less time than PGMO's surface correction alone.

We also evaluated ElasticFusion on TUM, its original benchmark, and on Kimera-Multi (R2-S3). ElasticFusion is a complete surfel SLAM system of the same class. WarpVox corrects the TSDF of an existing mapping pipeline using either RGB-D or LiDAR. Fusion then continues on the corrected TSDF (R1-3(b)).
\end{reply}
\point{Editor E3: controlled target ablation}{Isolate the benefit of the observation-derived targets through a controlled ablation.}
\begin{reply}
Table~V compares observation-derived targets with nearest-keyframe pose transport in place of Stages~1 and~2. The observation-derived targets improve F@25 by 0.637\,pp.

The TSDF was fused from the measurements of the keyframes that observed each surface point. Stage~1 uses the pre-correction poses to reassociate each vertex with those measurements and assign reliability weights. Stage~2 moves them with the corrected poses and combines them into the observation-derived target of Eq.~(4).

The controls in R1-3(a) and R1-3(c) clarify the contribution of each step:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,topsep=2pt,itemsep=2pt]
\item replacing only the measured samples of Eq.~(3) with pose transport keeps all but 0.042\,pp of the gain;
\item limiting each vertex to 1, 4, 8, or 15 observations ($n_{\max}$) changes F@25 by at most 0.019\,pp;
\item removing the Stage-1 gate and reliability cutoff drops F@25 below nearest-keyframe pose transport.
\end{itemize}
The largest effect comes from identifying which keyframes actually observed each vertex.
\end{reply}
\point{Editor E4: repeated correction and fusion}{Evaluate stability over repeated correction-and-fusion cycles.}
\begin{reply}
We tested repeated correction and fusion on five multi-loop-closure sequences, with 15 corrections in total (R1-3(b)). WarpVox maintains surface accuracy close to reintegration, and its corrected TSDF accepts new measurements after each correction. At each correction, the method reassociates surface vertices with the original keyframe measurements.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,topsep=2pt,itemsep=2pt]
\item F-scores are measured against ground truth. Right after a correction, WarpVox differs from reintegration by $+0.50$\,pp at F@25 on average, with no common trend over successive corrections.
\item Volumetric metrics use reintegration as the reference because no ground-truth TSDF is available. On the voxels observed in both TSDFs, the corrected TSDF stays close to reintegration. Successive corrections mainly change which voxels are observed in both.
\item Both methods fuse new measurements using the same Voxblox update. New measurements refine revisited regions; other regions retain their corrected state. Every fusion interval raises all four volumetric metrics, and after fusion $\Delta F@25$ is $-0.01$\,pp on average.
\end{itemize}
Figure~\ref{fig:reply-cycles} in R1-3(b) shows all 25 checkpoints.
\end{reply}

\textbf{GT-referenced surface scores (right axis).}
WarpVox achieves surface accuracy close to reintegration. $\Delta F@25$ is $+0.50$\,pp on average at AC and $-0.01$\,pp at AF. At AC, it ranges from $-1.48$ to $+2.55$\,pp and shows no common trend over successive corrections. Both methods use the same measurements and corrected poses but update the maps differently. WarpVox reassociates observed vertices with their original keyframe measurements and moves them under the corrected poses. Reintegration fuses those measurements again under the corrected poses. At AF, both maps have fused the same new frames using the same Voxblox update; their F-scores agree on average.

\textbf{Volumetric-consistency metrics (left axis).}
The four volumetric metrics compare the WarpVox and reintegrated TSDFs because no ground-truth TSDF exists. Sign accuracy stays above 84\% and free-space recall above 93\% at every checkpoint. Stage~4 preserves most source-TSDF signs and observed free space, contributing to these high values. Support IoU and weighted Jaccard measure whether both TSDFs contain the same observed voxels with similar weights. Successive corrections mainly change these two. After the last correction, support IoU is 72 -- 85\%.

These support metrics differ because WarpVox reconstructs the TSDF from the corrected surface, whereas reintegration fuses the full measurement history again. A TSDF records voxel observation status, integration weight, and signed distance in addition to its surface. The volumetric metrics capture differences in these quantities. Thus, improved surface accuracy against ground truth can coexist with lower volumetric agreement with reintegration. At the second correction of 1207\_sparkal1, for example, WarpVox is better than reintegration by 1.68\,pp at F@25, while its weighted Jaccard falls.

The corrected TSDF retains most source weights and supports subsequent Voxblox fusion updates. Continued fusion raises all four metrics in every interval. New frames increase agreement in revisited regions; other regions retain their corrected state. Across repeated correction-and-fusion cycles, WarpVox maintains surface accuracy close to reintegration while continuing to integrate new measurements.
\end{reply}

We use fixed parameters for each modality across all datasets and sequences. Parameters expressed as multiples of voxel size $s$ scale with grid resolution.

Section III-B gives the function for $\alpha_{rj}$, which R2-B3 repeats. Section III-C gives the aggregation weights and the trimming rule (Eq.~(4)). For observation $r$ of vertex $j$, the aggregation weight is
\[
\omega_{rj}=\begin{cases}
\alpha_{rj}/(1+z^m_{rj}/\zeta_m), & \text{RGB-D},\\
\alpha_{rj}/(1+\|q_{rj}^+-v_j^-\|/\zeta_m), & \text{LiDAR},
\end{cases}
\]
where $q_{rj}^+$ is the matched sensor sample moved with the corrected keyframe pose. The first target $\bar q_j$ is the weighted mean over the observations whose keyframes have a corrected pose. We keep the samples whose distance to $\bar q_j$ is at most $\max(\epsilon_m,\kappa\,\operatorname{median}_r\|q_{rj}^+-\bar q_j\|)$. Their weighted mean is the observation-derived target $v_j^*$ of Eq.~(4). If a vertex has fewer than three valid samples, we keep all of them. R2-D3 -- D4, R2-D11, and R2-D18 give the neighbourhood, rejection, and repair rules.

\point{R2-S2: Conceptual distinction from ElasticFusion}{In terms of the method, the authors do not demonstrate a clear conceptual novelty relative to ElasticFusion. ElasticFusion uses surfels to compute and apply ARAP deformations based on map-to-frame pose estimates. The difference seems to be the use of triangles in place of surfels for computing the deformations, and possibly the many clustering and filtering steps that are incomprehensible due to vague descriptions.}
\begin{reply}
The difference is not the use of triangles in place of surfels. It is where the correction comes from and what the correction returns.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,topsep=2pt,itemsep=2pt]
\item \emph{Where the correction comes from.} ElasticFusion derives its deformation constraints from local and global surface registration inside its own surfel SLAM system. So its map is corrected only by registrations that the system accepts itself. WarpVox takes the trajectory correction from a separate SLAM back-end. It associates the stored keyframe measurements with the fused surface under the old poses. It then moves them with the corrected poses to obtain the deformation targets.
\item \emph{What the correction returns.} ElasticFusion, Kintinuous, and Kimera-PGMO return corrected surfels, point clouds, or meshes. WarpVox returns a standard TSDF. The mesh is only an intermediate step. Stage~4 combines the corrected mesh with the weights, support, sign, and free space of the source TSDF. The mapper can then keep fusing into the result and apply later corrections (R1-3(b)). To our knowledge, WarpVox is the first deformation-based method that returns such a TSDF.
\end{itemize}
In the released ElasticFusion code, the option \texttt{-p} replaces per-frame tracking with given poses. Loop closure stays on, and its deformation constraints still come from ElasticFusion's own registrations. So a later revision of the trajectory cannot reach the already fused map. WarpVox provides this step for an existing TSDF.

Section III-D defines the clustering and filtering steps, Algorithm~1 gives their order, and Figure~4 illustrates them (R2-G2, R2-D1 -- D19).
\end{reply}
\point{R2-S3: ElasticFusion evaluation}{Although deformation-based approaches are not new, as is the case with ElasticFusion, the baselines are either reintegration-based or submap-based. Therefore, I suggest clarifying the conceptual difference to ElasticFusion, and comparing performance against ElasticFusion, rather than VoxBlox reintegration or VoxGraph.}
\begin{reply}
We agree that WarpVox should be compared with surface-deformation methods. R2-S2 gives the conceptual difference to ElasticFusion. In the submitted manuscript, we compared WarpVox with Kimera-PGMO under the same input mesh and trajectories to isolate the correction method.

ElasticFusion represents the methods that the reviewer asks about. As requested, we also ran ElasticFusion as a complete system, as in its own paper (Section IV-C). We used TUM fr3/long\_office\_household and ten Kimera-Multi sequences:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,topsep=2pt,itemsep=2pt]
\item TUM is ElasticFusion's own room-scale benchmark. Our ElasticFusion setup reaches an absolute trajectory error (ATE) RMSE of 1.80\,cm, close to the 1.7\,cm reported in Table~1 of the ElasticFusion paper. We evaluate at 1, 2, and 5\,cm against a reference reconstructed from RGB-D measurements with motion-capture poses. ElasticFusion obtains 62.77\% F@2 and 3.69\,cm Chamfer distance, against 72.13\% and 2.82\,cm for WarpVox, which corrects Voxblox with the ORB-SLAM3 trajectory as in Section IV-B.
\item On the larger Kimera-Multi sequences, we compare at the last evaluated loop event of each sequence. ElasticFusion's own tracking and loop closure accumulate a mean ATE of 115.8\,m, and its map reaches 1.98\% F@25.
\end{itemize}
These results compare whole systems. ElasticFusion corrects its map with its own registrations (R2-S2). WarpVox applies the trajectory correction of the SLAM system in use. ElasticFusion accepts no later trajectory revision, so the correction method itself is compared with Kimera-PGMO. Kimera-PGMO is a more recent representative of this class. Like ElasticFusion, it uses an embedded deformation graph, but it takes external trajectory corrections. WarpVox improves F@25/F@50 by 0.26/0.91\,pp and lowers the Chamfer distance from 179.0 to 152.9\,cm (Section IV-C). It also corrects the surface $2.34\times$ faster. WarpVox then rebuilds the TSDF in Stage~4. Even this full update takes 4.792\,s, less than PGMO's 8.231\,s. The rebuilt surface is still better than PGMO at F@25/F@50.

Voxblox reintegration and Voxgraph complement these comparisons in Section IV-B, because WarpVox returns a TSDF. Reintegration rebuilds the same kind of TSDF from the same measurements and corrected poses, so it serves as the reference. Voxgraph is the TSDF-based alternative. It corrects drift through rigid submaps.

WarpVox takes the trajectory correction of a separate SLAM system. It corrects the TSDF of an existing mapping pipeline for RGB-D and LiDAR alike, which suits the growing scale and sensing of today's mapping data.
\end{reply}

\subsection*{Sec. III-A}

\point{R2-A1}{How are TSDF and weight field represented? Regular 3D grid?}
\begin{reply}
Yes. Voxblox stores distance and weight at each voxel of a regular 3-D grid, allocated sparsely in blocks. Section III-A now states this representation explicitly.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-A2}{V\textasciicircum{}- and F are not defined.}
\begin{reply}
The submitted Section~III-A introduces $\mathcal M^-=(V^-,F)$. The revision explicitly identifies $V^-$ as the vertex positions and $F$ as triangles stored as vertex-index triples.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-A3}{What do "active TSDF cells" mean? Cells around the zero-level set?}
\begin{reply}
Yes. They are the Marching-Cubes cells that contribute triangles to $\mathcal M^-$, i.e., the cells that the zero-level set crosses. The submitted Section~III-A defines them, and the revision repeats this definition in the cost analysis.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-A4}{What does "modality-native" mean?}
\begin{reply}
RGB-D depth images and spherical LiDAR range images, as specified after the submitted Eq.~(1). The revision replaces ``modality-native'' with these explicit names.
\end{reply}

\subsection*{Sec. III-B}

\point{R2-B1}{What kind of spatial indexing scheme allows storing/retrieving visibility relationship between vertices and sensors?}
\begin{reply}
A $k$-d tree (RGB-D) or a grid (LiDAR) retrieves nearby keyframe centres under the pre-correction poses. The search radius is 7.2 -- 7.4\,m for RGB-D (the 5\,m viewing frustum, depending on camera intrinsics) and 25\,m for LiDAR. The index only retrieves candidates. Projection and the geometric gate then decide which keyframes observed the vertex (Section~III-B).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-B2}{What does it mean to "sample the projected depth"? Do you mean get the closest?}
\begin{reply}
For LiDAR, yes: we take the return closest in 3-D to the projected vertex, within a $3\times3$ pixel window. For RGB-D, we interpolate the depth bilinearly from the four pixels around the projected position. At least two of these pixels must be valid. We then back-project the depth along the same ray.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-B3}{What is the exact formula for association quality, \textbackslash{}alpha\_\{rj\}?}
\begin{reply}
The reliability is
\[
\alpha_{rj}=\frac{|\cos\phi_{rj}|}{1+z^-_{rj}/z_0}
\exp(-\delta_{rj}/\sigma_g)\exp(-|z^m_{rj}-z^-_{rj}|/\sigma_z).
\]
Here $\phi$ is the incidence angle, $\delta$ the 3-D correspondence residual, and $z^-,z^m$ the projected-vertex and measured depth/range. The last exponential applies to RGB-D only. Section III-B defines these quantities, and the parameter table gives the constants.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-B4}{What are the criteria for retaining a candidate? Can multiple candidates be retained? What if none are retained?}
\begin{reply}
A valid sample must pass the geometric gate and, for RGB-D, the reliability cutoff. Each vertex retains up to 15 candidates; with none, it follows the surface deformation without a measured target (Section~III-B).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-B5}{Is m\_\{rj\} a point or an image?}
\begin{reply}
$m_{rj}\in\mathbb R^3$ is the sampled sensor-frame point returned by $\mathcal S_m$ in the submitted Eq.~(2); $D_r$ is the range image.
\end{reply}

\subsection*{Sec. III-C}

\point{R2-C1}{What do RGB-D/LIDAR "records" mean? Are these just frames? Also, why are RGB-D associated with timestamp but LIDAR with poses?}
\begin{reply}
``Records'' refer to keyframe observations: RGB-D depth images or LiDAR scans. Both use the corresponding corrected pose; RGB-D retrieves it by timestamp, with interpolation when needed, and LiDAR by scan index (Section~III-C).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-C2}{What if there are no candidates r satisfying Eq. 5?}
\begin{reply}
Then the vertex receives no target and follows the Stage-3 deformation, like an unobserved vertex (Section~III-C). The threshold with $\kappa=2.5$ keeps at least half of any nonempty candidate set. So this happens only when none of the vertex's observations has a corrected pose. That is the case when all its keyframes lie outside the corrected trajectory or in an interpolation gap longer than three times the median spacing.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-C3}{What does "supported source vertex" mean?}
\begin{reply}
It is a vertex with at least one retained observation that has a corrected pose ($\mathcal O_j\neq\emptyset$ and $\mathcal J_j\neq\emptyset$; Sections~III-B and III-C). Stage~2 turns these observations into its target $v_j^*$ (Eq.~(4)), so the vertex becomes a measured control. Sections III-B to III-D now call such a vertex ``observed''.
\end{reply}

\subsection*{Sec. III-D}

\point{R2-D1}{What do "controls" mean?}
\begin{reply}
A control is a vertex whose position is prescribed in the ARAP solve. A measured control uses the target $v_j^*$ from Stage 2 (Section~III-D).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D2}{What do "connected components" mean? Are these connected components of the mesh? How are they tracked?}
\begin{reply}
These are connected components of the triangle mesh: vertices connected through mesh edges belong to the same component. Components are recomputed for the current source mesh at each correction, giving labels local to that event.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D3}{What does "component-restricted inverse-distance predictor" do? I.e. what significance does connectivity have in predicting inverse distance?}
\begin{reply}
It predicts displacement from neighbouring controls using inverse-squared-distance weights. Preferring neighbours on the same mesh component separates nearby surface copies caused by drift that may require different corrections (Section~III-D(a)).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D4}{What is a "median - MAD residual test"? Residual between what? What is MAD?}
\begin{reply}
The test compares a measured control displacement $d_j$ with the neighbourhood prediction $\hat d_j$. Over all tested controls, define
\[
e_j=\|d_j-\hat d_j\|,\qquad
\mu=\operatorname{median}_j e_j,\qquad
\mathrm{MAD}=\operatorname{median}_j|e_j-\mu|.
\]
A control is rejected when
\[
e_j>\mu+1.5\max(1.4826\,\mathrm{MAD},0.05\,\mathrm{m}).
\]
Here MAD is the median absolute deviation; Section III-D(a), the parameter table, and Algorithm 1 specify the test, settings, and execution order.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D5}{What does "statistically isolated control" mean? Do you mean outliers?}
\begin{reply}
Yes---outlier controls. The revision uses ``outlier'' and gives the test in Section III-D(a).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D6}{What is the difference between "omitted components" (due to being "supported only by rejected controls") and "Retained components without a surviving measured control"? I.e., how can components be retained without surviving "controls" if they are omitted?}
\begin{reply}
The difference is the component size. Small components (fragments, fewer than $N_{\rm f}=100$ faces) are omitted when they have no control, either from the start or after step (a) rejects all their controls. Larger components without a control are kept and receive synthetic controls in step (b).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D7}{Constraints were not yet defined here, yet there are descriptions of "receiving robust motion constraints"}
\begin{reply}
The constraint is the control displacement $d_j=v_j^*-v_j^-$, a prescribed vertex displacement. The revision defines it at the end of Stage~2 (Section III-C), before Stage~3 introduces synthetic controls.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D8}{What does "QEM" stand for?}
\begin{reply}
Quadric error metric (Garland and Heckbert, 1997), expanded in Section~III-D.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D9}{Eq. 7: is this the "control" or the corrected proxy vertex position?}
\begin{reply}
The submitted Eq.~(7) defines the initial proxy target: the proxy rest position plus the average displacement of its clustered controls (Section III-D(d)).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D10}{What does "controlled proxy vertex" mean? How are vertices controlled/uncontrolled?}
\begin{reply}
A proxy vertex is controlled when it has an assigned target, from mapped controls ($\mathcal K_a\neq\emptyset$) or from the three anchors added to an otherwise unconstrained component. The remaining vertex positions are optimized in the ARAP solve (Section~III-D(d) -- (e)).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D11}{What does "only when it is a gross residual" mean? What does it mean for residuals to be a strict majority? What are the "synthetic targets"?}
\begin{reply}
A \emph{gross residual} satisfies $r_a=\|p_a^{(0)}-\hat p_a\|\ge4.685\sigma_a$, where $\hat p_a$ is the local rigid prediction and $\sigma_a$ the robust fitting scale. A \emph{strict majority} means more than half the fitted measured targets in that component are gross. \emph{Synthetic targets} are assigned to anchor components without measured controls. Section III-D(d), Eq.~(5), specifies their update rule.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D12}{Eq. 8: p\_\{a\}\textasciicircum{}\{+\} and p\_b\textasciicircum{}\{+\} are not defined. Should these be p\_\{a\}\textasciicircum{}\{\textbackslash{}star\}? What is P\textasciicircum{}+? Why is it an optimization variable?}
\begin{reply}
No. $P^+=\{p_a^+\}$ are the deformed proxy positions that ARAP solves for, and $p_a^*$ are the targets that fix the controlled vertices. $P^+$ is a variable because the positions of the uncontrolled vertices are unknown. Section III-D(e) now defines both before Eq.~(6).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D13}{What does "proxy one-ring" mean?}
\begin{reply}
$\operatorname{star}_P(a)$ is the one-ring of proxy triangles incident to vertex $a$ (Section~III-D (e)).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D14}{Give the formula for "symmetric cotangent weights"}
\begin{reply}
$c_{bc}=\sum_{f\supset(b,c)}\tfrac12\cot\vartheta^f_{bc}$, where $\vartheta^f_{bc}$ is the angle opposite edge $(b,c)$ in incident rest triangle $f$ (Section~III-D(e)).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D15}{Is the proposed method using the "proxy" or the source mesh?}
\begin{reply}
We solve on the 60K QEM proxy and transfer its displacements to the source mesh. The submitted Section III-D specifies this standard configuration. Section IV-E compares it with full-mesh ARAP: F@25 stays within 0.032\,pp, and Stage~3 time drops from 3.323\,s to 1.408\,s.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D16}{What does "topology-local" mean?}
\begin{reply}
It means that a source vertex takes its displacement only from its own connected surface. The interpolating triangle is chosen among those incident to the vertex's own proxy vertex $\pi(j)$ (Section~III-D(f)).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D17}{What does "star(\textbackslash{}pi(j))" mean?}
\begin{reply}
$\operatorname{star}_P(\pi(j))$ is the set of proxy triangles incident to $\pi(j)$ (Section~III-D (f)).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D18}{How are "edge-stretch, area-change or aspect-ratio bounds" defined mathematically?}
\begin{reply}
Section III-D(f) defines
\[
\xi^{\rm e}=\max_e\frac{\ell_e^+}{\ell_e^-},\qquad
\xi^{\rm A}=\frac{A^+}{A^-},\qquad
\xi^{\rm asp}=\frac{(\ell^+_{\max})^2}{2A^+},
\]
where $\ell$ and $A$ are edge length and face area, and $-/+$ denote source/warped geometry. A face is flagged at $\xi^{\rm e}\ge3$, $\xi^{\rm A}\ge6$, or $\xi^{\rm asp}\ge20$ with $\ell^+_{\max}\ge0.5$\,m. These bounds are listed in the parameter table.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D19}{What does "observed support" mean?}
\begin{reply}
Observed source support is $\Omega^-=\{x:w^-(x)>0\}$. It contains voxels carrying integration evidence, including both the surface neighbourhood and observed free space. Section III-A separates this volumetric set from observed mesh vertices, which are defined by retained keyframe correspondences in Section III-B.
\end{reply}

\subsection*{Sec. III-E}

\point{R2-E1}{What is "surface-local reconstruction/domain"? Is there a "surface-global reconstruction/domain"?}
\begin{reply}
It is the part of Stage~4 that rebuilds the voxels within truncation distance $\tau$ of the warped surface. It visits only the blocks that contain a warped vertex, and their 26 neighbours. There is no surface-global counterpart. Away from the surface, the second part of Stage~4 transports the observed free space of the source TSDF.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E2}{The first paragraph says the "sign orientation" comes from the source mesh, whereas Eq. 12 claims it is "determined separately from the source field".}
\begin{reply}
Both are used, with different roles. The mesh normal gives the geometric orientation. Valid samples of the source TSDF decide which side is free space (positive). The Stage-4 text and Eq.~(9) now state this rule and its fallbacks.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E3}{What does "weight provides support" mean?}
\begin{reply}
A voxel with positive weight is observed (supported). Transferring the weight keeps both this support and its integration strength for later fusion (Section~III-E, Eq.~(8)).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E4}{What is the fallback branch?}
\begin{reply}
The fallback initializes voxels within $\rho=1.5s$ of the warped surface that have no transferred source weight. It uses the warped geometry and a prior weight in $[0,1]$, excluding open boundaries (Section~III-E).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E5}{What is a "consistent source sample"?}
\begin{reply}
A trilinear source sample is valid when every voxel with a nonzero interpolation coefficient has positive integration weight. It is sign-consistent when those voxels also share the same nonzero distance sign. Section III-E defines the two tests before Eq.~(8).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E6}{What is the difference between w\_\{tr\}\textasciicircum{}+ and w\_\{src\}\textasciicircum{}+?}
\begin{reply}
They are the same weight on the transferred voxels. The revision keeps only $w_{\rm src}^+$ and removes the submitted Eq.~(13). Eq.~(8) defines $w_{\rm src}^+$ once, and Eq.~(10) uses it on $\Omega_{\rm tr}$.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E7}{How is n\_f\textasciicircum{}+ defined?}
\begin{reply}
$n_f^+$ is the unit normal of warped face $f=(a,b,c)$, oriented by the vertex order of $F$; Section~III-E also defines the paired frames built from it (Eq.~(7)).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E8}{How does the algorithm determine if the closest surface feature is an open boundary?}
\begin{reply}
Boundary edges are incident to exactly one face. The prior is withheld when the closest point lies on such an edge or its end vertex. This keeps geometric fallback support away from open boundaries (Section~III-E, fallback paragraph).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E9}{What does "supported voxel" mean?}
\begin{reply}
A supported voxel has positive weight, $w>0$ (Section~III-A); $\Omega^+$ is the set of supported output voxels (Eq.~(10)).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E10}{What is a "fallback support"? What are "narrow samples"?}
\begin{reply}
\emph{Fallback support} is the set of voxels with positive fallback weight, $\Omega_{\rm fb}=\{x:w_{\rm fb}(x)>0\}$. \emph{Narrow samples} are transferred voxels within distance $\rho$ of the warped surface, now denoted $\mathcal N_{\rm tr}$ (Section~III-E).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E11}{What makes a source face valid or invalid?}
\begin{reply}
A source face is valid if neither it nor its warped copy is degenerate, so that its paired frames can be built. Each transport cell uses the source face closest to the centroid of its observed voxels, with no distance limit. A cell whose closest face is invalid is not transported (Section~III-E).
\end{reply}

\subsection*{Sec. III-F}

\point{R2-F1}{What are C\_\{def\} and C\_\{vol\}?}
\begin{reply}
$C_{\rm def}$ is the Stage-3 cost of deformation, including proxy construction, target consolidation, ARAP, and transfer or repair. $C_{\rm vol}$ is the Stage-4 cost of TSDF reconstruction and free-space transport (Section~III-F).
\end{reply}

\subsection*{Sec. IV-A}

\point{R2-IVA1}{Fully define F@10/25/50. Are these F1-score at 10/25/50cm thresholds?}
\begin{reply}
Yes. The submitted Section IV-A specifies bidirectional nearest-neighbour tests at 10/25/50\,cm. Explicitly, $F=100\cdot2PR/(P+R)$, where $P$ and $R$ are the fractions of predicted and reference surface points, respectively, within the threshold of the other surface.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-IVA2}{Please also add Chamfer distance, a standard metric for geometric similarity.}
\begin{reply}
We computed the symmetric, unsquared Chamfer distance for all reconstruction baselines and the PGMO comparison:
\[
\mathrm{CD}=\frac12\left(\frac{1}{|\mathcal P|}\sum_{p\in\mathcal P}d(p,\mathcal G)
+\frac{1}{|\mathcal G|}\sum_{g\in\mathcal G}d(g,\mathcal P)\right).
\]
Here $\mathcal P$ and $\mathcal G$ are the predicted and reference point sets, and $d(p,\mathcal G)$ is the nearest-neighbour distance. The table averages per-event, untruncated distances in centimetres over the same cases as the F-scores.
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lrr}\toprule
Method & Kimera-Multi (39) & KITTI (12)\\\midrule
Uncorrected & 247.7 & 126.2\\
Open3D & 114.9 & 82.9\\
Voxgraph & 142.2 & 124.8\\
Wavemap & 132.1 & 71.1\\
Voxblox reintegration & 101.9 & 67.3\\
WarpVox & 103.1 & 68.8\\\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
WarpVox stays within 1.5\,cm of reintegration on both datasets and below every other correction baseline. On the ten-sequence corrected-surface comparison (Table~IV), CD is 309.6\,cm for the input mesh and 152.9\,cm for WarpVox. Kimera-PGMO reaches 179.0\,cm with one-shot correction, the stronger of its two modes, and 183.8\,cm online.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-IVA3}{Table II shows inconsistent relative portions between different datasets. Please discuss why some datasets incur larger time in, e.g., association, whereas other datasets incur larger time in target estimation.}
\begin{reply}
Stage times depend on different workloads: association processes candidate vertex -- keyframe pairs, target construction processes retained observations, and deformation/reconstruction depend on surface and volume size. RGB-D queries every source vertex, whereas LiDAR queries one per occupied 0.5\,m cell and searches spherical-image neighbourhoods. These modality and map-size differences affect the stage proportions. 
\end{reply}

\point{R2-IVA4}{Table III: What are "ten bags"?}
\begin{reply}
A ``bag'' is the ROS recording of one robot's Kimera-Multi sequence. Table~IV compares the final states of ten such sequences (10/14: acl\_jackal2, sparkal1, sparkal2; 12/07: acl\_jackal2, hathor, sparkal1; 12/08: acl\_jackal2, apis, sobek, thoth). Section IV-A defines them as the set shared by the PGMO comparison, repeated correction, and ablation.
\end{reply}
