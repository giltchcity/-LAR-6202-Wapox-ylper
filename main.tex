\section*{Response to the Editor}
The Editor's report is grouped into the four requests below.
\point{Editor E1: clarity and reproducibility}{Substantial revisions are needed to improve methodological clarity and reproducibility. The revision should provide missing algorithmic details and parameters.}
\begin{reply}
The revised Section III follows how the map was formed:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,topsep=2pt,itemsep=2pt]
\item Stage~1 traces TSDF fusion backwards to the keyframe measurements behind each surface vertex;
\item Stage~2 places them under the corrected poses;
\item Stage~3 propagates these targets over the surface by ARAP on a proxy mesh;
\item Stage~4 restores a native TSDF from the corrected surface and the source weights, signs, and observed space.
\end{itemize}
The parameter table lists all settings, which are fixed within each modality across datasets and sequences, and Algorithm~1 orders the Stage-3 steps. Figures~3 -- 5, added to the Method and reproduced in Figure~\ref{fig:reply-mechanisms}, illustrate association, component-aware clustering, and output support, and R1-3(c) reports threshold sensitivity.
\end{reply}
\point{Editor E2: relation to deformation-based approaches}{Better establish the contribution relative to existing deformation-based approaches.}
\begin{reply}
WarpVox differs from existing deformation-based approaches in where the correction comes from and what it returns, not in the surface primitive (R2-S2): it applies trajectory corrections from an external SLAM back-end to an already fused TSDF and returns a native TSDF for continued fusion without full reintegration (Sections I -- II).

The comparison with surface-deformation methods is already part of the submitted evaluation: Section IV-C (Table~III) compares WarpVox with Kimera-PGMO, which corrects the trajectory and mesh jointly through an embedded deformation graph, and WarpVox achieves higher F@25/F@50 and lower Chamfer distance in less than half the surface-correction time. Its complete update, including native-TSDF reconstruction, is faster than PGMO's surface correction alone.

As requested by Reviewer 2, we additionally evaluated ElasticFusion, a self-contained surfel SLAM system of the same class, as a complete system on TUM, its own benchmark, and on Kimera-Multi (R2-S3); WarpVox corrects the TSDF of an existing mapping pipeline for RGB-D and LiDAR alike. R1-3(b) demonstrates continued fusion on the corrected volume.

Each component of the method is isolated under shared inputs: the observation-derived targets against Kimera-PGMO and nearest-keyframe pose transport (E3), and the source-supported TSDF against mesh-only conversion (Section IV-E) and over repeated correction and fusion (E4).
\end{reply}
\point{Editor E3: controlled target ablation}{Isolate the benefit of the observation-derived targets through a controlled ablation.}
\begin{reply}
Just as the TSDF is formed from the measurements of the keyframes that observed it, Stage~1 aims to recover and weight these observations for each vertex under the pre-correction poses, and Stage~2 places them under the corrected poses and aggregates them into the observation-derived targets of Eq.~(4). Table~IV, which replaces both stages with nearest-keyframe pose transport, isolates their benefit: 0.637 percentage points at F@25. The controls in R1-3(a) and R1-3(c) separate this benefit:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,topsep=2pt,itemsep=2pt]
\item replacing only the measured samples of Eq.~(3) with pose transport retains all but 0.042 points of it;
\item the observation cap $n_{\max}\in\{1,4,8,15\}$ changes F@25 by at most 0.019 points;
\item removing the Stage-1 gate and reliability screening drops F@25 below nearest-keyframe transport.
\end{itemize}
The gain comes mainly from the observations themselves, the keyframes that Stage-1 association and screening identify as having observed each vertex.
\end{reply}
\point{Editor E4: repeated correction and fusion}{Evaluate stability over repeated correction-and-fusion cycles.}
\begin{reply}
WarpVox remains stable in surface accuracy over repeated correction and fusion, on five multi-loop sequences with 15 corrections in total, and its TSDF supports continued fusion after every correction. Each correction recovers the observations again from the keyframe measurements, anchoring the surface to the original measurements.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,topsep=2pt,itemsep=2pt]

\textbf{Contribution of each step.}
The controls separate the steps: the measured samples of Eq.~(3) add 0.042 percentage points, screening adds 1.13 points, and the number of observations combined per vertex, capped by $n_{\max}\in\{1,4,8,15\}$, changes mean F@25 by at most 0.019 points. Screening is what makes the recovered observations reliable: removing the gate and reliability screening drops F@25 from 26.62\% to 25.49\%, below the 25.98\% of nearest-keyframe transport, while the gate, cutoff, and trimming settings change mean F@25 by at most 0.19\,pp (R1-3(c)). The gain comes mainly from the observations themselves, the keyframes that Stage-1 association and screening identify as having observed each vertex.

\textbf{Why the observations carry the gain.}
Stages~1 and~2 trace TSDF fusion backwards. Projective TSDF fusion integrates each sample into the voxels along its pixel ray within the truncation band; Stage~1 applies this rule from the surface back to the keyframes, with a gate tighter than the truncation band. Fusion forms the surface at a vertex as a weighted combination of the measurements that observed it. Stage~1 recovers these measurements by projective association, keeps those consistent with the surface by the geometric gate, and weights them by reliability; Stage~2 places them under the corrected poses with Eq.~(3) and combines them by the trimmed weighted mean of Eq.~(4). The two factors named in the review are steps of this construction.

The requested baseline is not an existing method: it keeps WarpVox's Stage-1 association, gate, and reliability weights and replaces only the measured samples of Eq.~(3) with pose transport. The comparison locates the main gain in the association-based construction, which nearest-keyframe transport and Kimera-PGMO's timestamp-linked deformation graph lack; WarpVox exceeds the first by 0.637 percentage points at F@25 and the second at F@25/F@50 and in Chamfer distance (Section IV-C of the revision).
\end{reply}

The F-scores compare each map with the ground truth. All settings are fixed: within each modality, the same values are used for every dataset and sequence, and settings expressed as multiples of the voxel size $s$ scale with the grid resolution.

For observation $r$ of vertex $j$, the aggregation weight is
\[
\omega_{rj}=\begin{cases}
\alpha_{rj}/(1+z^m_{rj}/\zeta_m), & \text{RGB-D},\\
\alpha_{rj}/(1+\|q_{rj}^+-v_j^-\|/\zeta_m), & \text{LiDAR},
\end{cases}
\]
where $q_{rj}^+$ is the matched sensor sample transformed by the corrected keyframe pose. The initial target $\bar q_j$ is its weighted mean over observations with valid corrected poses. We retain samples whose residual from $\bar q_j$ is at most $\max(\epsilon_m,\kappa\,\operatorname{median}_r\|q_{rj}^+-\bar q_j\|)$ and recompute the weighted mean, which gives the observation-derived target $v_j^*$ of Eq.~(4). With fewer than three valid samples, all are retained. R2-D3 -- D4 and R2-D11 specify the neighbourhood and robust rejection rules; R2-D18 gives the repair tests.

We varied the RGB-D association gate $g_0$ (0.04 and 0.16\,m) and reliability cutoff $\alpha_{\min}$ (0 and 0.20), the Stage-2 trimming parameters $\kappa\in\{1.5,4.0\}$ and $\epsilon_m\in\{0.01,0.05\}$\,m, and the Stage-3 MAD multiplier, scale floor, neighbourhood, Welsch cutoff, and repair settings one at a time on the ten ablation cases. \point{R2-S2: Conceptual distinction from ElasticFusion}{The difference seems to be the use of triangles in place of surfels for computing the deformations.}
\begin{reply}
WarpVox and ElasticFusion share the idea of correcting a fused map by deforming its surface; the distinction lies in where the correction comes from and what it returns, not in the surface primitive. WarpVox corrects an externally maintained TSDF after a trajectory revision supplied by a separate SLAM back-end and returns a native TSDF for continued fusion (Sections I -- II). Stored keyframe measurements are associated with the fused surface under the old poses and repositioned under the corrected poses to construct deformation targets. The mesh is an intermediate representation: reconstruction combines its corrected geometry with source support, sign, integration weights, and free space to restore the volumetric state needed for fusion.

ElasticFusion derives deformation constraints from local and global surface registration within its surfel SLAM system, so its map is corrected only by registrations that the system accepts itself. In the released code, the option \texttt{-p} replaces per-frame tracking with given poses, while loop closure stays enabled and its deformation constraints still come from ElasticFusion's own registrations. A later revision of the trajectory has no route to the already fused map, which is the operation WarpVox provides for an existing TSDF.

Kimera-PGMO, like WarpVox, propagates trajectory corrections, but to a timestamped mesh rather than a TSDF; like ElasticFusion, it uses an embedded deformation graph. The submitted Table III compares WarpVox with Kimera-PGMO on the same input mesh with PGMO's own optimized trajectory, isolating the correction method: WarpVox improves F@25/F@50 by 0.26/0.91 percentage points and lowers Chamfer distance from 179.0 to 152.9\,cm, with a $2.34\times$ speedup in surface correction. WarpVox then reconstructs the native TSDF in Stage 4; the complete update takes 4.792\,s, below PGMO's 8.231\,s, and the reconstructed surface exceeds PGMO at F@25/F@50. R1-3(b) demonstrates subsequent fusion and correction on WarpVox-corrected maps.
\end{reply}
\point{R2-S3: ElasticFusion evaluation}{I suggest clarifying the conceptual difference to ElasticFusion, and comparing performance against ElasticFusion, rather than VoxBlox reintegration or VoxGraph.}
\begin{reply}
We agree that WarpVox should also be compared with surface-deformation methods. ElasticFusion represents the methods that the reviewer asks about, and the submitted Section IV-C already compares WarpVox with a more recent representative of this class, Kimera-PGMO, which also uses an embedded deformation graph, on the same input mesh and trajectories (Table III, R2-S2). WarpVox improves F@25/F@50 by 0.26/0.91 percentage points and lowers Chamfer distance from 179.0 to 152.9\,cm in less than half the time. As requested, we additionally evaluated ElasticFusion as a complete system, as in its published evaluation, on TUM fr3/long\_office\_household and ten Kimera-Multi sequences.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,topsep=2pt,itemsep=2pt]
\item TUM is ElasticFusion's own room-scale benchmark. Our ElasticFusion setup reaches an absolute trajectory error (ATE) RMSE of 1.80\,cm, close to the 1.7\,cm reported in Table 1 of the ElasticFusion paper. Evaluated at 1, 2, and 5\,cm against a reference reconstructed from RGB-D measurements with motion-capture poses, ElasticFusion obtains 62.77\% F@2 and 3.69\,cm Chamfer distance, against 72.13\% and 2.82\,cm for WarpVox, which corrects Voxblox with the ORB-SLAM3 trajectory as in Section IV-B.
\item On the larger Kimera-Multi sequences, compared at the last evaluated loop event in each sequence, ElasticFusion's own tracking and loop closure accumulate a mean ATE of 115.8\,m, leaving its map at 1.98\% F@25.
\end{itemize}
This result reflects the system-level difference: ElasticFusion deforms its map through its own registrations (R2-S2), whereas WarpVox applies the trajectory revisions of the SLAM system in use. Since ElasticFusion accepts no later trajectory revision, the correction method itself is compared with Kimera-PGMO, which applies an embedded deformation graph, as ElasticFusion does, to external trajectory corrections under shared inputs and trajectories (above).

The method contribution lies in two components, each isolated under shared inputs:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,topsep=2pt,itemsep=2pt]
\item the observation-derived targets correct the surface more accurately than Kimera-PGMO's deformation graph (above) and exceed nearest-keyframe pose transport by 0.637 percentage points at F@25, mainly through the observations themselves (R1-3(a));

\end{itemize}

ElasticFusion is a self-contained surfel SLAM system that corrects its map through the registrations of its own tracking (R2-S2). WarpVox takes the trajectory correction of a separate SLAM system and corrects the TSDF of an existing mapping pipeline for RGB-D and LiDAR alike, which suits the growing scale and sensing of today's mapping data. Section IV-C of the revision reports this comparison.
\end{reply}

\subsection*{Sec. III-A}

\point{R2-A1}{How are TSDF and weight field represented? Regular 3D grid?}
\begin{reply}
Yes. Voxblox stores distance and weight at each voxel of a regular 3-D grid, allocated sparsely in blocks. Section III-A now states this representation explicitly.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-A2}{V\textasciicircum{}- and F are not defined.}
\begin{reply}
The submitted Sec.~III-A introduces $\mathcal M^-=(V^-,F)$. The revision explicitly identifies $V^-$ as the vertex positions and $F$ as triangles stored as vertex-index triples.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-A3}{What do "active TSDF cells" mean? Cells around the zero-level set?}
\begin{reply}
These are the Marching-Cubes cells contributing triangles to $\mathcal M^-$, as introduced in the submitted Sec.~III-A. The revision repeats this definition in the cost analysis.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-A4}{What does "modality-native" mean?}
\begin{reply}
RGB-D depth images and spherical LiDAR range images, as specified after the submitted Eq.~(1). The revision replaces ``modality-native'' with these explicit names.
\end{reply}

\subsection*{Sec. III-B}

\point{R2-B1}{What kind of spatial indexing scheme allows storing/retrieving visibility relationship between vertices and sensors?}
\begin{reply}
A $k$-d tree (RGB-D) or grid (LiDAR) retrieves nearby keyframe centres under the pre-correction poses, within 14.3 -- 14.7\,m (the 10\,m viewing frustum, depending on camera intrinsics) or 80\,m, respectively. Projection and geometric verification establish the vertex -- measurement associations (Sec.~III-B); the index retrieves spatial candidates.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-B2}{What does it mean to "sample the projected depth"? Do you mean get the closest?}
\begin{reply}
For RGB-D, the depth is bilinearly interpolated from the four pixels around the projected position, with at least two valid pixels, and back-projected along the same ray. For LiDAR, the stored return closest in 3-D to the projected source vertex is selected within a $3\times3$ pixel window.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-B3}{What is the exact formula for association quality, \textbackslash{}alpha\_\{rj\}?}
\begin{reply}
The reliability is
\[
\alpha_{rj}=\frac{|\cos\phi_{rj}|}{1+z^-_{rj}/z_0}
\exp(-\delta_{rj}/\sigma_g)\exp(-|z^m_{rj}-z^-_{rj}|/\sigma_z).
\]
Here $\phi$ is the incidence angle, $\delta$ the 3-D correspondence residual, and $z^-,z^m$ the projected-vertex and measured depth/range. The last exponential applies to RGB-D only. Section III-B defines these quantities, and the parameter table gives the constants.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-B4}{What are the criteria for retaining a candidate? Can multiple candidates be retained? What if none are retained?}
\begin{reply}
A valid sample must pass the geometric gate and, for RGB-D, the reliability cutoff. Each vertex retains up to 15 candidates; with none, it follows the surface deformation without a measured target (Sec.~III-B).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-B5}{Is m\_\{rj\} a point or an image?}
\begin{reply}
$m_{rj}\in\mathbb R^3$ is the sampled sensor-frame point returned by $\mathcal S_m$ in the submitted Eq.~(2); $D_r$ is the range image.
\end{reply}

\subsection*{Sec. III-C}

\point{R2-C1}{What do RGB-D/LIDAR "records" mean? Are these just frames? Also, why are RGB-D associated with timestamp but LIDAR with poses?}
\begin{reply}
``Records'' refer to keyframe observations: RGB-D depth images or LiDAR scans. Both use the corresponding corrected pose; RGB-D retrieves it by timestamp, with interpolation when needed, and LiDAR by scan index (Sec.~III-C).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-C2}{What if there are no candidates r satisfying Eq. 5?}
\begin{reply}
For a nonempty candidate set, the median-based threshold with $\kappa=2.5$ retains at least half the candidates. $\mathcal J_j$ is empty only when none of the vertex's observations has a corrected pose, i.e., all its keyframes fall outside the corrected trajectory or within an interpolation gap above three times the median spacing. Such a vertex is treated as unobserved: it receives no target and follows the Stage-3 deformation (Sec.~III-C).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-C3}{What does "supported source vertex" mean?}
\begin{reply}
A supported (observed) vertex has at least one retained observation with a corrected pose, $\mathcal O_j\neq\emptyset$ and $\mathcal J_j\neq\emptyset$ (Secs.~III-B and III-C); Stage~2 turns these observations into one target $v_j^*$ (Eq.~(4)), which makes it a measured control. Sections III-B to III-D use ``observed vertex'' for this condition.
\end{reply}

\subsection*{Sec. III-D}

\point{R2-D1}{What do "controls" mean?}
\begin{reply}
A control is a vertex whose position is prescribed in the ARAP solve; a measured control uses the target $v_j^*$ from Stage 2 (Sec.~III-D).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D2}{What do "connected components" mean? Are these connected components of the mesh? How are they tracked?}
\begin{reply}
These are connected components of the triangle mesh: vertices connected through mesh edges belong to the same component. Components are recomputed for the current source mesh at each correction, giving labels local to that event.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D3}{What does "component-restricted inverse-distance predictor" do? I.e. what significance does connectivity have in predicting inverse distance?}
\begin{reply}
It predicts displacement from neighbouring controls using inverse-squared-distance weights. Preferring neighbours on the same mesh component separates nearby surface copies caused by drift that may require different corrections (Sec.~III-D(a)).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D4}{What is a "median - MAD residual test"? Residual between what? What is MAD?}
\begin{reply}
The test compares a measured control displacement $d_j$ with the neighbourhood prediction $\hat d_j$. Over all tested controls, define
\[
e_j=\|d_j-\hat d_j\|,\qquad
\mu=\operatorname{median}_j e_j,\qquad
\mathrm{MAD}=\operatorname{median}_j|e_j-\mu|.
\]
A control is rejected when
\[
e_j>\mu+1.5\max(1.4826\,\mathrm{MAD},0.05\,\mathrm{m}).
\]
Here MAD is the median absolute deviation; Section III-D(a), the parameter table, and Algorithm 1 specify the test, settings, and execution order.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D5}{What does "statistically isolated control" mean? Do you mean outliers?}
\begin{reply}
Yes---outlier controls. The revision uses ``outlier'' and gives the test in Section III-D(a).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D6}{What is the difference between "omitted components" (due to being "supported only by rejected controls") and "Retained components without a surviving measured control"? I.e., how can components be retained without surviving "controls" if they are omitted?}
\begin{reply}
Fragments have fewer than $N_{\rm f}=100$ faces. Control-free fragments are removed during preprocessing; fragments that lose all controls in step (a) are also omitted. The remaining components without controls receive synthetic controls in step (b).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D7}{Constraints were not yet defined here, yet there are descriptions of "receiving robust motion constraints"}
\begin{reply}
The constraint is the control displacement $d_j=v_j^*-v_j^-$, a prescribed vertex displacement; the revision defines it at the end of Stage~2 (Section III-C), before Stage~3 introduces synthetic controls.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D8}{What does "QEM" stand for?}
\begin{reply}
Quadric error metric (Garland and Heckbert, 1997), expanded in Sec.~III-D.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D9}{Eq. 7: is this the "control" or the corrected proxy vertex position?}
\begin{reply}
The submitted Eq.~(7) defines the initial proxy target: the proxy rest position plus the average displacement of its clustered controls (Section III-D(d) in the revision).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D10}{What does "controlled proxy vertex" mean? How are vertices controlled/uncontrolled?}
\begin{reply}
A proxy vertex is controlled when it has an assigned target, from mapped controls ($\mathcal K_a\neq\emptyset$) or from the three anchors added to an otherwise unconstrained component. The remaining vertex positions are optimized in the ARAP solve (Sec.~III-D(d) -- (e)).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D11}{What does "only when it is a gross residual" mean? What does it mean for residuals to be a strict majority? What are the "synthetic targets"?}
\begin{reply}
A \emph{gross residual} satisfies $r_a=\|p_a^{(0)}-\hat p_a\|\ge4.685\sigma_a$, where $\hat p_a$ is the local rigid prediction and $\sigma_a$ the robust fitting scale. A \emph{strict majority} means more than half the fitted measured targets in that component are gross. \emph{Synthetic targets} are assigned to anchor components without measured controls. Section III-D(d), Eq.~(5), specifies their update rule.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D12}{Eq. 8: p\_\{a\}\textasciicircum{}\{+\} and p\_b\textasciicircum{}\{+\} are not defined. Should these be p\_\{a\}\textasciicircum{}\{\textbackslash{}star\}? What is P\textasciicircum{}+? Why is it an optimization variable?}
\begin{reply}
$P^+=\{p_a^+\}$ are the deformed proxy positions. The targets $p_a^*$ prescribe the controlled positions; the remaining positions are optimized by ARAP. Section III-D(e) now defines both before Eq.~(6).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D13}{What does "proxy one-ring" mean?}
\begin{reply}
$\operatorname{star}_P(a)$ is the one-ring of proxy triangles incident to vertex $a$ (Sec.~III-D (e)).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D14}{Give the formula for "symmetric cotangent weights"}
\begin{reply}
$c_{bc}=\sum_{f\supset(b,c)}\tfrac12\cot\vartheta^f_{bc}$, where $\vartheta^f_{bc}$ is the angle opposite edge $(b,c)$ in incident rest triangle $f$ (Sec.~III-D(e)).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D15}{Is the proposed method using the "proxy" or the source mesh?}
\begin{reply}
We solve on the 60K QEM proxy and transfer its displacements to the source mesh. The submitted Section III-D specifies this standard configuration, and Table IV compares it with full-mesh ARAP.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D16}{What does "topology-local" mean?}
\begin{reply}
The interpolating triangle is selected from those incident to the vertex's own proxy vertex $\pi(j)$, keeping displacement transfer on the same connected surface (Sec.~III-D(f)).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D17}{What does "star(\textbackslash{}pi(j))" mean?}
\begin{reply}
$\operatorname{star}_P(\pi(j))$ is the set of proxy triangles incident to $\pi(j)$ (Sec.~III-D (f)).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D18}{How are "edge-stretch, area-change or aspect-ratio bounds" defined mathematically?}
\begin{reply}
Section III-D(f) defines
\[
\xi^{\rm e}=\max_e\frac{\ell_e^+}{\ell_e^-},\qquad
\xi^{\rm A}=\frac{A^+}{A^-},\qquad
\xi^{\rm asp}=\frac{(\ell^+_{\max})^2}{2A^+},
\]
where $\ell$ and $A$ are edge length and face area, and $-/+$ denote source/warped geometry. A face is flagged at $\xi^{\rm e}\ge3$, $\xi^{\rm A}\ge6$, or $\xi^{\rm asp}\ge20$ with $\ell^+_{\max}\ge0.5$\,m. These bounds are listed in the parameter table.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D19}{What does "observed support" mean?}
\begin{reply}
Observed source support is $\Omega^-=\{x:w^-(x)>0\}$. It contains voxels carrying integration evidence, including both the surface neighbourhood and observed free space. Section III-A separates this volumetric set from observed mesh vertices, which are defined by retained keyframe correspondences in Section III-B.
\end{reply}

\subsection*{Sec. III-E}

\point{R2-E1}{What is "surface-local reconstruction/domain"? Is there a "surface-global reconstruction/domain"?}
\begin{reply}
Surface-local reconstruction processes voxel centres within truncation distance $\tau$ of the warped surface, restricted to blocks containing a warped vertex and their 26 neighbours. The second part of Stage 4 transports supported source free space.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E2}{The first paragraph says the "sign orientation" comes from the source mesh, whereas Eq. 12 claims it is "determined separately from the source field".}
\begin{reply}
The source TSDF provides the sign orientation: the mesh normal defines geometric orientation, and valid source-field samples establish which side is positive free space. The revised Stage-4 opening and Eq.~(9) state this rule and its fallbacks explicitly.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E3}{What does "weight provides support" mean?}
\begin{reply}
Positive TSDF weight denotes observed support. Transferring this weight retains both that support and its integration strength for subsequent fusion (Sec.~III-E, Eq.~(8)).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E4}{What is the fallback branch?}
\begin{reply}
The fallback initializes voxels within $\rho=1.5s$ of the warped surface that have no transferred source weight. It uses the warped geometry and a prior weight in $[0,1]$, excluding open boundaries (Sec.~III-E).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E5}{What is a "consistent source sample"?}
\begin{reply}
A trilinear source sample is valid when every voxel with a nonzero interpolation coefficient has positive integration weight. It is sign-consistent when those voxels also share the same nonzero distance sign. Section III-E defines the two tests before Eq.~(8).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E6}{What is the difference between w\_\{tr\}\textasciicircum{}+ and w\_\{src\}\textasciicircum{}+?}
\begin{reply}
The earlier $w_{\rm tr}^+$ duplicated $w_{\rm src}^+$ on the transferred-voxel set. We removed the redundant symbol and the former Eq.~(13). Equation (8) defines the source weight once, and Eq.~(10) uses it directly on $\Omega_{\rm tr}$.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E7}{How is n\_f\textasciicircum{}+ defined?}
\begin{reply}
$n_f^+$ is the unit normal of warped face $f=(a,b,c)$, oriented by the vertex order of $F$; Sec.~III-E also defines the paired frames built from it (Eq.~(7)).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E8}{How does the algorithm determine if the closest surface feature is an open boundary?}
\begin{reply}
Boundary edges are incident to exactly one face. The prior is withheld when the closest point lies on such an edge or its end vertex, keeping geometric fallback support away from open boundaries (Sec.~III-E, fallback paragraph).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E9}{What does "supported voxel" mean?}
\begin{reply}
A supported voxel has positive weight, $w>0$ (Sec.~III-A); $\Omega^+$ is the set of supported output voxels (Eq.~(10)).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E10}{What is a "fallback support"? What are "narrow samples"?}
\begin{reply}
\emph{Fallback support} is the set of voxels with positive fallback weight, $\Omega_{\rm fb}=\{x:w_{\rm fb}(x)>0\}$. \emph{Narrow samples} are transferred voxels within distance $\rho$ of the warped surface, denoted $\mathcal N_{\rm tr}$ in the revision (Sec.~III-E).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E11}{What makes a source face valid or invalid?}
\begin{reply}
Each transport cell takes the source face closest to the centroid of its observed voxels; there is no distance limit. The face is valid if neither it nor its warped copy is degenerate, so that its paired frames can be constructed; a cell whose closest face is invalid is not transported (Sec.~III-E).
\end{reply}

\subsection*{Sec. III-F}

\point{R2-F1}{What are C\_\{def\} and C\_\{vol\}?}
\begin{reply}
$C_{\rm def}$ is the Stage-3 cost of deformation, including proxy construction, target consolidation, ARAP, and transfer or repair. $C_{\rm vol}$ is the Stage-4 cost of TSDF reconstruction and free-space transport (Sec.~III-F).
\end{reply}

\subsection*{Sec. IV-A}

\point{R2-IVA1}{Fully define F@10/25/50. Are these F1-score at 10/25/50cm thresholds?}
\begin{reply}
Yes. The submitted Section IV-A specifies bidirectional nearest-neighbour tests at 10/25/50\,cm. Explicitly, $F=100\cdot2PR/(P+R)$, where $P$ and $R$ are the fractions of predicted and reference surface points, respectively, within the threshold of the other surface.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-IVA2}{Please also add Chamfer distance, a standard metric for geometric similarity.}
\begin{reply}
We computed the symmetric, unsquared Chamfer distance for all reconstruction baselines and the PGMO comparison:
\[
\mathrm{CD}=\frac12\left(\frac{1}{|\mathcal P|}\sum_{p\in\mathcal P}d(p,\mathcal G)
+\frac{1}{|\mathcal G|}\sum_{g\in\mathcal G}d(g,\mathcal P)\right).
\]
Here $\mathcal P$ and $\mathcal G$ are the predicted and reference point sets, and $d(p,\mathcal G)$ is the nearest-neighbour distance. The table averages per-event, untruncated distances in centimetres over the same cases as the F-scores.
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lrr}\toprule
Method & Kimera-Multi (39) & KITTI (12)\\\midrule
Uncorrected & 247.7 & 126.2\\
Open3D & 114.9 & 82.9\\
Voxgraph & 142.2 & 124.8\\
Wavemap & 132.1 & 71.1\\
Voxblox reintegration & 101.9 & 67.3\\
WarpVox & 103.1 & 68.8\\\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
WarpVox stays within 1.5\,cm of reintegration on both datasets and below every other correction baseline. On the ten-sequence corrected-surface comparison (Table III), CD is 309.6\,cm for the input mesh, 179.0\,cm for Kimera-PGMO one-shot correction, the stronger of its two modes (183.8\,cm online), and 152.9\,cm for WarpVox. WarpVox reduces CD by 26.1\,cm relative to one-shot PGMO under shared inputs.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-IVA3}{Table II shows inconsistent relative portions between different datasets. Please discuss why some datasets incur larger time in, e.g., association, whereas other datasets incur larger time in target estimation.}
\begin{reply}
Stage times depend on different workloads: association processes candidate vertex -- keyframe pairs, target construction processes retained observations, and deformation/reconstruction depend on surface and volume size. RGB-D queries every source vertex, whereas LiDAR queries one per occupied 0.5\,m cell and searches spherical-image neighbourhoods. These modality and map-size differences affect the stage proportions. 
\end{reply}

\point{R2-IVA4}{Table III: What are "ten bags"?}
\begin{reply}
A ``bag'' is the ROS recording of one robot's Kimera-Multi sequence. Table III compares the final states of ten such sequences (10/14: acl\_jackal2, sparkal1, sparkal2; 12/07: acl\_jackal2, hathor, sparkal1; 12/08: acl\_jackal2, apis, sobek, thoth), which Section IV-A of the revision defines as the set shared by the PGMO comparison, repeated correction, and ablation.
\end{reply}
