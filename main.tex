\section*{Response to the Editor}
We group the Editor's report into four requests.
\point{Editor E1: clarity and reproducibility}{Substantial revisions are needed to improve methodological clarity and reproducibility. The revision should provide missing algorithmic details and parameters.}
\begin{reply}
Section III now defines the requested terms and update rules. Table~I lists the parameters, and Algorithm~1 specifies the order of the Stage-3 steps. Figures~3 -- 5 illustrate association, clustering, and the support of the output TSDF. We use one fixed parameter set per modality (RGB-D or LiDAR) across datasets and sequences. The presentation follows the four stages:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,topsep=2pt,itemsep=2pt]
\item Stage~1 identifies the keyframe measurements that observed each surface vertex;
\item Stage~2 transforms those measurements with the corrected poses and aggregates them into a target;
\item Stage~3 deforms a proxy mesh toward the targets using ARAP;
\item Stage~4 reconstructs a standard TSDF using the corrected surface and the source TSDF's weights, signs, and observed space.
\end{itemize}
The replies to Reviewer~2 identify the corresponding definitions; R1-3(c) reports threshold sensitivity.
\end{reply}
\point{Editor E2: relation to deformation-based approaches}{Better establish the contribution relative to existing deformation-based approaches.}
\begin{reply}
WarpVox differs from existing deformation-based methods in the source of its correction and the representation it produces (R2-S2). ElasticFusion, Kintinuous, and Kimera-PGMO deform surfels, point clouds, or meshes. WarpVox takes the corrected trajectory from an external SLAM back-end. It corrects a fused TSDF without full reintegration and returns a standard TSDF that supports continued fusion. To our knowledge, it is the first deformation-based method to return such a TSDF (Sections I -- II).

In the submitted manuscript, we compared WarpVox with Kimera-PGMO (Section IV-C). Kimera-PGMO corrects the trajectory and the mesh together with an embedded deformation graph. With the same input mesh and trajectories, WarpVox achieves higher F@25/F@50 and a lower Chamfer distance while taking less than half the surface-correction time. The full WarpVox update, including TSDF reconstruction, also takes less time than PGMO's surface correction alone.

We also evaluated ElasticFusion on TUM, its original benchmark, and on Kimera-Multi (R2-S3). ElasticFusion is a complete surfel SLAM system of the same class. WarpVox corrects the TSDF of an existing mapping pipeline using either RGB-D or LiDAR. Fusion then continues on the corrected TSDF (R1-3(b)).
\end{reply}
\point{Editor E3: controlled target ablation}{Isolate the benefit of the observation-derived targets through a controlled ablation.}
\begin{reply}
Table~V compares observation-derived targets with nearest-keyframe pose transport in place of Stages~1 and~2. The observation-derived targets improve F@25 by 0.637\,pp.

The TSDF was fused from the measurements of the keyframes that observed each surface point. Stage~1 uses the pre-correction poses to reassociate each vertex with those measurements and assign reliability weights. Stage~2 moves them with the corrected poses and combines them into the observation-derived target of Eq.~(4).

The controls in R1-3(a) and R1-3(c) clarify the contribution of each step:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,topsep=2pt,itemsep=2pt]
\item replacing only the measured samples of Eq.~(3) with pose transport keeps all but 0.042\,pp of the gain;
\item limiting each vertex to 1, 4, 8, or 15 observations ($n_{\max}$) changes F@25 by at most 0.019\,pp;
\item removing the Stage-1 gate and reliability cutoff drops F@25 below nearest-keyframe pose transport.
\end{itemize}
The largest effect comes from identifying which keyframes actually observed each vertex.
\end{reply}
\point{Editor E4: repeated correction and fusion}{Evaluate stability over repeated correction-and-fusion cycles.}
\begin{reply}
We tested repeated correction and fusion on five multi-loop-closure sequences, with 15 corrections in total (R1-3(b)). WarpVox maintains surface accuracy close to reintegration, and its corrected TSDF accepts new measurements after each correction. At each correction, the method reassociates surface vertices with the original keyframe measurements.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,topsep=2pt,itemsep=2pt]
\item F-scores are measured against ground truth. Right after a correction, WarpVox differs from reintegration by $+0.50$\,pp at F@25 on average, with no common trend over successive corrections.
\item Volumetric metrics use reintegration as the reference because no ground-truth TSDF is available. On the voxels observed in both TSDFs, the corrected TSDF stays close to reintegration. Successive corrections mainly change which voxels are observed in both.
\item Both methods fuse new measurements using the same Voxblox update. New measurements refine revisited regions; other regions retain their corrected state. Every fusion interval raises all four volumetric metrics, and after fusion $\Delta F@25$ is $-0.01$\,pp on average.
\end{itemize}
Figure~\ref{fig:reply-cycles} in R1-3(b) shows all 25 checkpoints.
\end{reply}

\textbf{GT-referenced surface scores (right axis).}
WarpVox achieves surface accuracy close to reintegration. $\Delta F@25$ is $+0.50$\,pp on average at AC and $-0.01$\,pp at AF. At AC, it ranges from $-1.48$ to $+2.55$\,pp and shows no common trend over successive corrections. Both methods use the same measurements and corrected poses but update the maps differently. WarpVox reassociates observed vertices with their original keyframe measurements and moves them under the corrected poses. Reintegration fuses those measurements again under the corrected poses. At AF, both maps have fused the same new frames using the same Voxblox update; their F-scores agree on average.

\textbf{Volumetric-consistency metrics (left axis).}
The four volumetric metrics compare the WarpVox and reintegrated TSDFs because no ground-truth TSDF exists. Sign accuracy stays above 84\% and free-space recall above 93\% at every checkpoint. Stage~4 preserves most source-TSDF signs and observed free space, contributing to these high values. Support IoU and weighted Jaccard measure whether both TSDFs contain the same observed voxels with similar weights. Successive corrections mainly change these two. After the last correction, support IoU is 72 -- 85\%.

These support metrics differ because WarpVox reconstructs the TSDF from the corrected surface, whereas reintegration fuses the full measurement history again. A TSDF records voxel observation status, integration weight, and signed distance in addition to its surface. The volumetric metrics capture differences in these quantities. Thus, improved surface accuracy against ground truth can coexist with lower volumetric agreement with reintegration. At the second correction of 1207\_sparkal1, for example, WarpVox is better than reintegration by 1.68\,pp at F@25, while its weighted Jaccard falls.

The corrected TSDF retains most source weights and supports subsequent Voxblox fusion updates. Continued fusion raises all four metrics in every interval. New frames increase agreement in revisited regions; other regions retain their corrected state. Across repeated correction-and-fusion cycles, WarpVox maintains surface accuracy close to reintegration while continuing to integrate new measurements.
\end{reply}

We use fixed parameters for each modality across all datasets and sequences. Parameters expressed as multiples of voxel size $s$ scale with grid resolution.

Section III-B gives the function for $\alpha_{rj}$, which R2-B3 repeats. Section III-C gives the aggregation weights and the trimming rule (Eq.~(4)). For observation $r$ of vertex $j$, the aggregation weight is
\[
\omega_{rj}=\begin{cases}
\alpha_{rj}/(1+z^m_{rj}/\zeta_m), & \text{RGB-D},\\
\alpha_{rj}/(1+\|q_{rj}^+-v_j^-\|/\zeta_m), & \text{LiDAR},
\end{cases}
\]
where $q_{rj}^+$ is the matched sensor sample moved with the corrected keyframe pose. The first target $\bar q_j$ is the weighted mean over the observations whose keyframes have a corrected pose. We keep the samples whose distance to $\bar q_j$ is at most $\max(\epsilon_m,\kappa\,\operatorname{median}_r\|q_{rj}^+-\bar q_j\|)$. Their weighted mean is the observation-derived target $v_j^*$ of Eq.~(4). If a vertex has fewer than three valid samples, we keep all of them. R2-D3 -- D4, R2-D11, and R2-D18 give the neighbourhood, rejection, and repair rules.

\point{R2-S2: Conceptual distinction from ElasticFusion}{In terms of the method, the authors do not demonstrate a clear conceptual novelty relative to ElasticFusion. ElasticFusion uses surfels to compute and apply ARAP deformations based on map-to-frame pose estimates. The difference seems to be the use of triangles in place of surfels for computing the deformations, and possibly the many clustering and filtering steps that are incomprehensible due to vague descriptions.}
\begin{reply}
The distinction lies in the source of deformation constraints and the resulting map representation, rather than the choice of triangles over surfels.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,topsep=2pt,itemsep=2pt]
\item \emph{Where the correction comes from.} ElasticFusion derives deformation constraints from local and global surface registration within its surfel SLAM system; map correction therefore depends on its own accepted registrations. WarpVox instead receives a corrected trajectory from an external SLAM back-end. It associates stored keyframe measurements with the fused surface under the original poses, then transforms those measurements using the corrected poses to construct deformation targets.
\item \emph{What the correction returns.} ElasticFusion, Kintinuous, and Kimera-PGMO return corrected surfels, point clouds, or meshes. WarpVox returns a standard TSDF. The mesh serves as an intermediate representation. Stage~4 combines the corrected mesh with the weights, support, sign, and free space of the source TSDF. The resulting volume supports continued fusion and subsequent corrections (R1-3(b)). To our knowledge, WarpVox is the first deformation-based method that returns such a TSDF.
\end{itemize}
In the released ElasticFusion code, \texttt{-p} supplies external per-frame poses but leaves loop closure enabled; deformation constraints still originate from ElasticFusion's own registrations. The system therefore does not apply a later external trajectory revision to an already fused map. WarpVox provides this step for an existing TSDF.

Section III-D defines clustering and filtering, Algorithm~1 specifies their order, and Figure~4 illustrates the process (R2-G2, R2-D1 -- D19).
\end{reply}
\point{R2-S3: ElasticFusion evaluation}{Although deformation-based approaches are not new, as is the case with ElasticFusion, the baselines are either reintegration-based or submap-based. Therefore, I suggest clarifying the conceptual difference to ElasticFusion, and comparing performance against ElasticFusion, rather than VoxBlox reintegration or VoxGraph.}
\begin{reply}
We compare WarpVox with surface-deformation methods in two settings. Kimera-PGMO uses the same input mesh and trajectories, isolating the correction method; R2-S2 explains the conceptual distinction from ElasticFusion.

We also evaluated ElasticFusion as a complete SLAM system, following its original evaluation setting (Section IV-C). We used TUM fr3/long\_office\_household and ten Kimera-Multi sequences:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,topsep=2pt,itemsep=2pt]
\item TUM is a room-scale benchmark used in the original ElasticFusion paper. Our ElasticFusion setup reaches an absolute trajectory error (ATE) RMSE of 1.80\,cm, close to the 1.7\,cm reported in Table~1 of the ElasticFusion paper. We evaluate at 1, 2, and 5\,cm thresholds against a reference reconstructed from RGB-D measurements using motion-capture poses. ElasticFusion obtains 62.77\% F@2 and 3.69\,cm Chamfer distance, against 72.13\% and 2.82\,cm for WarpVox, which corrects Voxblox with the ORB-SLAM3 trajectory as in Section IV-B.
\item On Kimera-Multi, we compare the final evaluated loop-closure event of each sequence. ElasticFusion's own tracking and loop closure accumulate a mean ATE of 115.8\,m, and its map reaches 1.98\% F@25.
\end{itemize}
These are whole-system comparisons: ElasticFusion corrects its map from its own registrations (R2-S2), whereas WarpVox applies corrections from an external SLAM trajectory. Because ElasticFusion does not incorporate subsequent external trajectory revisions, we use Kimera-PGMO for the controlled comparison of correction methods. Kimera-PGMO also uses an embedded deformation graph but accepts external trajectory corrections. WarpVox improves F@25/F@50 by 0.26/0.91\,pp and lowers the Chamfer distance from 179.0 to 152.9\,cm (Section IV-C). WarpVox's surface correction is $2.34\times$ faster. Including Stage-4 TSDF reconstruction, its full update takes 4.792\,s, compared with 8.231\,s for PGMO surface correction alone. The rebuilt surface is still better than PGMO at F@25/F@50.

Section IV-B adds Voxblox reintegration and Voxgraph as TSDF-based comparisons. Reintegration provides the reference by rebuilding the same type of TSDF from the same measurements and corrected poses. Voxgraph corrects drift through rigid TSDF submaps.

WarpVox applies corrections from a separate SLAM system to the TSDF of an existing RGB-D or LiDAR mapping pipeline.
\end{reply}

\subsection*{Sec. III-A}

\point{R2-A1}{How are TSDF and weight field represented? Regular 3D grid?}
\begin{reply}
Voxblox stores a signed distance and integration weight per voxel on a regular 3-D grid, with sparse block allocation. Section III-A now states this representation explicitly.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-A2}{V\textasciicircum{}- and F are not defined.}
\begin{reply}
Section III-A now defines $\mathcal M^-=(V^-,F)$, where $V^-$ contains vertex positions and $F$ contains triangular faces represented by vertex-index triples.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-A3}{What do "active TSDF cells" mean? Cells around the zero-level set?}
\begin{reply}
Active TSDF cells are Marching-Cubes cells crossed by the zero-level set that contribute triangles to $\mathcal M^-$. Section III-A and the cost analysis now use this explicit definition.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-A4}{What does "modality-native" mean?}
\begin{reply}
The term refers to RGB-D depth images and spherical LiDAR range images. We replace ``modality-native'' with these explicit terms.
\end{reply}

\subsection*{Sec. III-B}

\point{R2-B1}{What kind of spatial indexing scheme allows storing/retrieving visibility relationship between vertices and sensors?}
\begin{reply}
A $k$-d tree for RGB-D or a spatial grid for LiDAR retrieves nearby keyframe centres using pre-correction poses. The search radius is 7.2 -- 7.4\,m for RGB-D (the 5\,m viewing frustum, depending on camera intrinsics) and 25\,m for LiDAR. The index retrieves candidate keyframes; projection and the geometric gate determine whether each candidate observed the vertex (Section~III-B).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-B2}{What does it mean to "sample the projected depth"? Do you mean get the closest?}
\begin{reply}
For LiDAR, we select the 3-D return closest to the projected vertex within a $3\times3$-pixel window. For RGB-D, we bilinearly interpolate depth from the four surrounding pixels, requiring at least two valid values, then back-project along the corresponding ray.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-B3}{What is the exact formula for association quality, \textbackslash{}alpha\_\{rj\}?}
\begin{reply}
The association reliability is
\[
\alpha_{rj}=\frac{|\cos\phi_{rj}|}{1+z^-_{rj}/z_0}
\exp(-\delta_{rj}/\sigma_g)\exp(-|z^m_{rj}-z^-_{rj}|/\sigma_z).
\]
Here $\phi$ is the incidence angle, $\delta$ the 3-D correspondence residual, and $z^-,z^m$ the projected-vertex and measured depth/range. The final exponential applies only to RGB-D. Section III-B defines the variables, and Table~I lists the constants.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-B4}{What are the criteria for retaining a candidate? Can multiple candidates be retained? What if none are retained?}
\begin{reply}
A sample must pass the geometric gate and, for RGB-D, the reliability cutoff. Each vertex retains at most 15 candidates. A vertex with none receives no measured target and follows the Stage-3 surface deformation (Section~III-B).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-B5}{Is m\_\{rj\} a point or an image?}
\begin{reply}
$m_{rj}\in\mathbb R^3$ denotes the sampled point in the sensor frame, returned by $\mathcal S_m$ in Eq.~(2) of the submission; $D_r$ denotes the range image.
\end{reply}

\subsection*{Sec. III-C}

\point{R2-C1}{What do RGB-D/LIDAR "records" mean? Are these just frames? Also, why are RGB-D associated with timestamp but LIDAR with poses?}
\begin{reply}
``Records'' means keyframe observations: RGB-D depth images or LiDAR scans. Both use corrected poses. RGB-D retrieves poses by timestamp, interpolating when needed; LiDAR retrieves them by scan index (Section~III-C).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-C2}{What if there are no candidates r satisfying Eq. 5?}
\begin{reply}
A vertex with no surviving candidates receives no target and follows the Stage-3 deformation (Section~III-C). With $\kappa=2.5$, the trimming threshold retains at least half of a nonempty candidate set. Thus, this case arises only when none of the vertex's observations has a corrected pose. This can occur when all associated keyframes lie outside the corrected trajectory or within an interpolation gap longer than three times the median spacing.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-C3}{What does "supported source vertex" mean?}
\begin{reply}
A supported source vertex has at least one retained observation with a corrected pose ($\mathcal O_j\neq\emptyset$ and $\mathcal J_j\neq\emptyset$; Sections~III-B and III-C). Stage~2 aggregates these observations into target $v_j^*$ (Eq.~(4)), making the vertex a measured control. Sections III-B to III-D now call such a vertex ``observed''.
\end{reply}

\subsection*{Sec. III-D}

\point{R2-D1}{What do "controls" mean?}
\begin{reply}
A control is a vertex with a prescribed position in the ARAP solve. A measured control uses the Stage-2 target $v_j^*$ (Section~III-D).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D2}{What do "connected components" mean? Are these connected components of the mesh? How are they tracked?}
\begin{reply}
Connected components are sets of mesh vertices joined by paths along triangle edges. We recompute them on the source mesh at each correction; their labels are local to that event.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D3}{What does "component-restricted inverse-distance predictor" do? I.e. what significance does connectivity have in predicting inverse distance?}
\begin{reply}
The predictor estimates displacement from neighbouring controls using inverse-squared-distance weights. Restricting neighbours to the same mesh component prevents nearby but disconnected surfaces, such as drift-induced duplicates, from sharing a displacement estimate (Section~III-D(a)).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D4}{What is a "median - MAD residual test"? Residual between what? What is MAD?}
\begin{reply}
The test compares each measured-control displacement $d_j$ with its neighbourhood prediction $\hat d_j$. Across tested controls, define
\[
e_j=\|d_j-\hat d_j\|,\qquad
\mu=\operatorname{median}_j e_j,\qquad
\mathrm{MAD}=\operatorname{median}_j|e_j-\mu|.
\]
A control is rejected when
\[
e_j>\mu+1.5\max(1.4826\,\mathrm{MAD},0.05\,\mathrm{m}).
\]
MAD denotes median absolute deviation. Section III-D(a), Table~I, and Algorithm~1 specify the test, parameters, and execution order.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D5}{What does "statistically isolated control" mean? Do you mean outliers?}
\begin{reply}
The term refers to outlier controls. The revision uses ``outlier'' and defines the rejection test in Section III-D(a).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D6}{What is the difference between "omitted components" (due to being "supported only by rejected controls") and "Retained components without a surviving measured control"? I.e., how can components be retained without surviving "controls" if they are omitted?}
\begin{reply}
The distinction is component size. We omit fragments with fewer than $N_{\rm f}=100$ faces if they have no surviving control, whether initially or after step (a) rejects their controls. Larger components without a surviving control remain in the mesh and receive synthetic controls in step (b).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D7}{Constraints were not yet defined here, yet there are descriptions of "receiving robust motion constraints"}
\begin{reply}
A motion constraint prescribes the displacement $d_j=v_j^*-v_j^-$ of a control vertex. Section III-C now defines it at the end of Stage~2, before Stage~3 introduces synthetic controls.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D8}{What does "QEM" stand for?}
\begin{reply}
Quadric error metric (Garland and Heckbert, 1997), expanded in Section~III-D.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D9}{Eq. 7: is this the "control" or the corrected proxy vertex position?}
\begin{reply}
Eq.~(7) of the submission defines an initial proxy target, not the corrected proxy position: the proxy rest position plus the mean displacement of its clustered controls (Section III-D(d)).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D10}{What does "controlled proxy vertex" mean? How are vertices controlled/uncontrolled?}
\begin{reply}
A proxy vertex is controlled if it receives a target from mapped controls ($\mathcal K_a\neq\emptyset$) or from one of three anchors added to an otherwise unconstrained component. The ARAP solve optimizes the positions of all other vertices (Section~III-D(d) -- (e)).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D11}{What does "only when it is a gross residual" mean? What does it mean for residuals to be a strict majority? What are the "synthetic targets"?}
\begin{reply}
A \emph{gross residual} satisfies $r_a=\|p_a^{(0)}-\hat p_a\|\ge4.685\sigma_a$, where $\hat p_a$ is the local rigid prediction and $\sigma_a$ the robust fitting scale. A \emph{strict majority} requires more than half of the fitted measured targets in a component to have gross residuals. \emph{Synthetic targets} anchor components without measured controls. Section III-D(d), Eq.~(5), specifies their update rule.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D12}{Eq. 8: p\_\{a\}\textasciicircum{}\{+\} and p\_b\textasciicircum{}\{+\} are not defined. Should these be p\_\{a\}\textasciicircum{}\{\textbackslash{}star\}? What is P\textasciicircum{}+? Why is it an optimization variable?}
\begin{reply}
$P^+=\{p_a^+\}$ denotes the deformed proxy positions optimized by ARAP; $p_a^*$ denotes prescribed targets for controlled vertices. The positions of uncontrolled vertices are optimization variables. Section III-D(e) now defines both before Eq.~(6).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D13}{What does "proxy one-ring" mean?}
\begin{reply}
$\operatorname{star}_P(a)$ is the one-ring of proxy triangles incident to vertex $a$ (Section~III-D (e)).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D14}{Give the formula for "symmetric cotangent weights"}
\begin{reply}
$c_{bc}=\sum_{f\supset(b,c)}\tfrac12\cot\vartheta^f_{bc}$, where $\vartheta^f_{bc}$ is the angle opposite edge $(b,c)$ in incident rest triangle $f$ (Section~III-D(e)).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D15}{Is the proposed method using the "proxy" or the source mesh?}
\begin{reply}
We solve on a QEM proxy capped at 60,000 vertices and transfer the resulting displacements to the source mesh. Section III-D specifies this configuration. Section IV-E compares proxy and full-mesh ARAP: F@25 differs by at most 0.032\,pp, while Stage~3 time falls from 3.323\,s to 1.408\,s.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D16}{What does "topology-local" mean?}
\begin{reply}
A source vertex receives displacement only from its own connected surface. Interpolation uses a triangle incident to that source vertex's mapped proxy vertex $\pi(j)$ (Section~III-D(f)).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D17}{What does "star(\textbackslash{}pi(j))" mean?}
\begin{reply}
$\operatorname{star}_P(\pi(j))$ is the set of proxy triangles incident to $\pi(j)$ (Section~III-D (f)).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D18}{How are "edge-stretch, area-change or aspect-ratio bounds" defined mathematically?}
\begin{reply}
Section III-D(f) defines the deformation-quality measures
\[
\xi^{\rm e}=\max_e\frac{\ell_e^+}{\ell_e^-},\qquad
\xi^{\rm A}=\frac{A^+}{A^-},\qquad
\xi^{\rm asp}=\frac{(\ell^+_{\max})^2}{2A^+},
\]
where $\ell$ and $A$ are edge length and face area, and $-/+$ denote source/warped geometry. A face is flagged at $\xi^{\rm e}\ge3$, $\xi^{\rm A}\ge6$, or $\xi^{\rm asp}\ge20$ with $\ell^+_{\max}\ge0.5$\,m. Table~I lists these thresholds.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D19}{What does "observed support" mean?}
\begin{reply}
Observed source support is $\Omega^-=\{x:w^-(x)>0\}$. It includes voxels with integration evidence near the surface and in observed free space. This volumetric support differs from observed mesh vertices, which Section III-B defines using retained keyframe correspondences.
\end{reply}

\subsection*{Sec. III-E}

\point{R2-E1}{What is "surface-local reconstruction/domain"? Is there a "surface-global reconstruction/domain"?}
\begin{reply}
Surface-local reconstruction is the Stage-4 step that rebuilds voxels within truncation distance $\tau$ of the warped surface. It visits blocks containing warped vertices and their 26 neighbouring blocks; no surface-global reconstruction step is used. Away from the surface, Stage~4 transports the source TSDF's observed free space.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E2}{The first paragraph says the "sign orientation" comes from the source mesh, whereas Eq. 12 claims it is "determined separately from the source field".}
\begin{reply}
Both contribute: warped-mesh normals establish geometric orientation, while valid source-TSDF samples determine which side is free space (positive). Section III-E and Eq.~(9) specify this rule and its fallback cases.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E3}{What does "weight provides support" mean?}
\begin{reply}
A voxel is observed (supported) when its integration weight is positive. Weight transport preserves both observation support and integration strength for subsequent fusion (Section~III-E, Eq.~(8)).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E4}{What is the fallback branch?}
\begin{reply}
The fallback initializes voxels within $\rho=1.5s$ of the warped surface where no source weight is transferred. It derives their values from the warped geometry with a prior weight in $[0,1]$ and excludes open boundaries (Section~III-E).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E5}{What is a "consistent source sample"?}
\begin{reply}
A trilinear source sample is valid if every voxel with a nonzero interpolation coefficient has positive integration weight. It is sign-consistent if those voxels also have the same nonzero signed-distance sign. Section III-E states both tests before Eq.~(8).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E6}{What is the difference between w\_\{tr\}\textasciicircum{}+ and w\_\{src\}\textasciicircum{}+?}
\begin{reply}
Both symbols referred to the same weight on transferred voxels. We now use only $w_{\rm src}^+$: Eq.~(8) defines it, Eq.~(10) uses it on $\Omega_{\rm tr}$, and the redundant submitted Eq.~(13) has been removed.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E7}{How is n\_f\textasciicircum{}+ defined?}
\begin{reply}
$n_f^+$ denotes the unit normal of warped face $f=(a,b,c)$, oriented by the vertex ordering in $F$. Section III-E defines the corresponding paired frames in Eq.~(7).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E8}{How does the algorithm determine if the closest surface feature is an open boundary?}
\begin{reply}
An edge is a boundary edge if exactly one face is incident to it. We withhold the prior when the closest surface point lies on a boundary edge or either endpoint. This excludes geometric fallback support near open boundaries (Section~III-E, fallback paragraph).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E9}{What does "supported voxel" mean?}
\begin{reply}
A supported voxel has positive integration weight ($w>0$; Section III-A). The set of supported output voxels is $\Omega^+$ (Eq.~(10)).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E10}{What is a "fallback support"? What are "narrow samples"?}
\begin{reply}
\emph{Fallback support} comprises voxels with positive fallback weight, $\Omega_{\rm fb}=\{x:w_{\rm fb}(x)>0\}$. \emph{Narrow samples}, now denoted $\mathcal N_{\rm tr}$, are transferred voxels within distance $\rho$ of the warped surface (Section~III-E).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E11}{What makes a source face valid or invalid?}
\begin{reply}
A source face is valid if both it and its warped counterpart are nondegenerate, allowing their paired frames to be constructed. Each transport cell uses the source face nearest the centroid of its observed voxels, without a distance cutoff. Cells whose nearest face is invalid are not transported (Section~III-E).
\end{reply}

\subsection*{Sec. III-F}

\point{R2-F1}{What are C\_\{def\} and C\_\{vol\}?}
\begin{reply}
$C_{\rm def}$ denotes the Stage-3 deformation cost (proxy construction, target consolidation, ARAP, and displacement transfer or repair). $C_{\rm vol}$ denotes the Stage-4 cost (TSDF reconstruction and free-space transport) (Section~III-F).
\end{reply}

\subsection*{Sec. IV-A}

\point{R2-IVA1}{Fully define F@10/25/50. Are these F1-score at 10/25/50cm thresholds?}
\begin{reply}
F@10/25/50 are F1-scores computed from bidirectional nearest-neighbour tests at 10, 25, and 50\,cm, as specified in Section IV-A. Specifically, $F=100\cdot2PR/(P+R)$, where $P$ and $R$ are the fractions of predicted and reference surface points within the specified distance of the other surface, respectively.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-IVA2}{Please also add Chamfer distance, a standard metric for geometric similarity.}
\begin{reply}
We added the symmetric, unsquared Chamfer distance for every reconstruction baseline and the PGMO comparison:
\[
\mathrm{CD}=\frac12\left(\frac{1}{|\mathcal P|}\sum_{p\in\mathcal P}d(p,\mathcal G)
+\frac{1}{|\mathcal G|}\sum_{g\in\mathcal G}d(g,\mathcal P)\right).
\]
Here $\mathcal P$ and $\mathcal G$ denote predicted and reference point sets, and $d(p,\mathcal G)$ is the nearest-neighbour distance from $p$ to $\mathcal G$. The table reports mean per-event, untruncated distances in centimetres over the same cases used for the F-scores.
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lrr}\toprule
Method & Kimera-Multi (39) & KITTI (12)\\\midrule
Uncorrected & 247.7 & 126.2\\
Open3D & 114.9 & 82.9\\
Voxgraph & 142.2 & 124.8\\
Wavemap & 132.1 & 71.1\\
Voxblox reintegration & 101.9 & 67.3\\
WarpVox & 103.1 & 68.8\\\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
WarpVox differs from reintegration by at most 1.5\,cm on each dataset and has a lower Chamfer distance than the other correction baselines. On the ten-sequence corrected-surface comparison (Table~IV), CD is 309.6\,cm for the input mesh and 152.9\,cm for WarpVox. Kimera-PGMO reaches 179.0\,cm in its stronger one-shot mode and 183.8\,cm in online mode.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-IVA3}{Table II shows inconsistent relative portions between different datasets. Please discuss why some datasets incur larger time in, e.g., association, whereas other datasets incur larger time in target estimation.}
\begin{reply}
The stages have different cost drivers: association processes candidate vertex--keyframe pairs, target construction processes retained observations, and deformation and reconstruction depend on mesh and volume size. RGB-D queries every source vertex; LiDAR queries one vertex per occupied 0.5\,m cell and searches spherical-image neighbourhoods. Thus, a sequence with many candidate pairs can spend more time on association even when relatively few observations survive, whereas more retained observations increase target-construction cost. Map size and sensing modality further change the relative stage times. 
\end{reply}

\point{R2-IVA4}{Table III: What are "ten bags"?}
\begin{reply}
A ``bag'' denotes one robot's recorded ROS sequence in Kimera-Multi. Table~IV compares the final states of ten such sequences (10/14: acl\_jackal2, sparkal1, sparkal2; 12/07: acl\_jackal2, hathor, sparkal1; 12/08: acl\_jackal2, apis, sobek, thoth). Section IV-A identifies this ten-sequence set as the one shared by the PGMO comparison, repeated-correction experiment, and ablation studies.
\end{reply}
