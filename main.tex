\section*{Response to the Editor}
The Editor's report is grouped into the four requests below.
\point{Editor E1: clarity and reproducibility}{Substantial revisions are needed to improve methodological clarity and reproducibility. The revision should provide missing algorithmic details and parameters.}
\begin{reply}
The revised Section III follows how the map was formed: Stage~1 traces TSDF fusion backwards to the keyframe measurements behind each surface vertex, Stage~2 places them under the corrected poses, Stage~3 propagates these targets over the surface by ARAP on a proxy mesh, and Stage~4 restores a native TSDF from the corrected surface and the source weights, signs, and observed space. The parameter table lists all settings, which are fixed within each modality across datasets and sequences, and Algorithm~1 orders the Stage-3 steps. Figure~\ref{fig:reply-mechanisms}, also added to the Method, illustrates association, component-aware clustering, and output support, and R1-3(c) reports threshold sensitivity.
\end{reply}
\point{Editor E2: controlled target ablation}{Isolate the benefit of the observation-derived targets through a controlled ablation.}
\begin{reply}
The observation-derived targets are produced by Stages~1 and~2 together, which simulate Voxblox fusion in reverse, and their benefit is reported in the ablation of Table~IV: on the ten ablation cases, they raise F@25 by 0.637 percentage points over nearest-keyframe pose transport (26.62\% vs.\ 25.98\%). The matched multi-keyframe pose-transport baseline requested in R1-3(a) further ablates Stage~2: it keeps the candidate keyframes and reliability weights of Stage~1, replaces the candidate-target construction of Eq.~(3) by the keyframe's pre-to-post pose correction of the source vertex, and performs on par with the measured-sample targets (0.042 points lower, in eight of the ten cases). The benefit therefore comes from the observation-verified multi-keyframe association, and the target formula itself is equivalent up to the gated residual. R1-3(c) reports the association ablation.
\end{reply}
\point{Editor E3: repeated correction and fusion}{Evaluate stability over repeated correction-and-fusion cycles.}
\begin{reply}
Across repeated transfers of distances, support, and weights, the corrected TSDF remains ready for continued fusion, as three results show. Surface accuracy stays on par with reintegration: over five multi-loop sequences with 15 corrections, mean $\Delta F@25$ is $+0.36$\,pp after correction and $-0.01$\,pp after continued fusion, with no deterioration trend consistent across sequences. Most support and weight remain in the same voxels as in reintegration, with mean support IoU of 83.45\% and weighted Jaccard of 80.09\% immediately after correction. The corrected TSDF also receives new measurements correctly: in all ten continued-fusion intervals tested (1.44--599.03\,s), all four volumetric-consistency metrics increase. Since no ground-truth TSDF exists, these metrics compare WarpVox with the reintegrated volume voxel by voxel at identical coordinates; their trend across corrections reflects deviation from the reintegrated voxel state within this comparable domain, not a decay of map quality. R1-3(b) reports all 25 checkpoints.
\end{reply}
\point{Editor E4: relation to deformation-based approaches}{Better establish the contribution relative to existing deformation-based approaches.}
\begin{reply}
WarpVox differs from existing deformation-based approaches in where the correction comes from and what it returns, not in the surface primitive (R2-S2): it applies trajectory corrections from an external SLAM back-end to an already fused TSDF and returns a native TSDF for continued fusion without full reintegration (Sections I--II). The comparison with surface-deformation methods is already part of the submitted evaluation: Section IV-C (Table~III) compares WarpVox with Kimera-PGMO, which corrects the trajectory and mesh jointly through an embedded deformation graph, and WarpVox achieves higher F@25/F@50 and lower Chamfer distance in less than half the surface-correction time, while its complete update, including native-TSDF reconstruction, is faster than PGMO's surface correction alone. ElasticFusion, the representative named by Reviewer 2, extends this comparison: on TUM, its own benchmark, WarpVox leads it at every F-score threshold, and on Kimera-Multi its own tracking accumulates a mean ATE of 115.8\,m (R2-S3). R1-3(b) demonstrates continued fusion on the corrected volume.
\end{reply}

\textbf{What the control retains.}
In WarpVox, the candidate keyframes and reliability weights are determined by the measured samples. Stage~1 projects each source vertex into a candidate keyframe, samples the measurement $m_{rj}$, and retains the keyframe only if this sample passes the geometric gate $\delta_{rj}\le g_0+g_1z_{rj}^-$ and, for RGB-D, the reliability cutoff $\alpha_{\min}$; the reliability weight $\alpha_{rj}$ depends on the same sample through $\delta_{rj}$ and $|z^m_{rj}-z^-_{rj}|$. Keeping these keyframes and weights fixed therefore keeps the observation support in both variants. Both sets of targets are observation-derived; the baseline changes only the candidate-target construction of Eq.~(3), on an association that Stage~1 has already verified.

\textbf{Stage~2 as the continuation of Stage~1.}
Stage~1 performs this association under the pre-correction poses, and Stage~2 moves the vertex to where the associated measurements lie under the corrected poses. Matched pose transport instead moves the source vertex, as if each measurement lay exactly at it. With $m_{rj}$ the measurement and $\hat v_{rj}^-$ the source vertex in the same pre-correction keyframe frame,
\[
\begin{aligned}
q_{rj}^{\mathrm{meas}} &= \widehat R_r^+ m_{rj}+\widehat t_r^+,\\
q_{rj}^{\mathrm{pose}} &= \widehat R_r^+\hat v_{rj}^-+\widehat t_r^+,\\
\bigl\|q_{rj}^{\mathrm{meas}}-q_{rj}^{\mathrm{pose}}\bigr\|
&=\bigl\|\widehat R_r^+(m_{rj}-\hat v_{rj}^-)\bigr\|=\delta_{rj}\le g_0+g_1z_{rj}^-,
\end{aligned}
\]
where $(\widehat R_r^+,\widehat t_r^+)$ is the corrected keyframe pose. The two candidates thus differ only by the association residual that Stage~1 has already bounded; for RGB-D the bound is at most 0.18\,m over the 10\,m association range, within one 0.25\,m voxel. This bound explains the scale of the differences in Table~\ref{tab:reply-r1a-paired}, where the measured-sample targets score higher in eight of the ten cases.

\textbf{Where the benefit of the observation-derived targets appears.}
Of the 0.637-point gain over Nearest-KF (Table~IV), the measured-sample targets contribute 0.042 points and the observation-verified multi-keyframe association shared with the baseline the remaining 0.60 points (association ablation in R1-3(c)).
\end{reply}
\point{R1-3(b): repeated correction and fusion}{Section IV-A already describes sequential processing with fusion resumed from the corrected volume, and Section IV-D reports volumetric consistency. However, the averages do not show how discrepancies evolve over successive correction-and-fusion cycles within a sequence. For a few existing sequences with multiple loop closures, please report the difference in GT-referenced surface scores relative to full reintegration, together with the existing volumetric-consistency metrics, over successive events. Checkpoints immediately after correction and after a specified interval of additional fusion would help distinguish the two stages. The reference should use the same measurement history and corrected poses. Since distance values are reconstructed and source-derived support and weights are transferred repeatedly, this analysis would help establish whether the approximation remains stable during continued use. Exact equivalence with reintegration is not required.}
\begin{reply}
Continued fusion on the corrected TSDF is supported by three results: surface accuracy stays on par with reintegration, most support and weight remain in the same voxels as in reintegration (mean support IoU of 83.45\% and weighted Jaccard of 80.09\% after correction), and in all ten continued-fusion intervals tested, all four volumetric-consistency metrics increase. Surface accuracy shows no deterioration trend consistent across sequences.

We evaluated repeated correction and continued fusion on the five multi-loop sequences in the ten-sequence ablation set, covering two to six loop closures per sequence and 15 corrections. Each correction replaces the active TSDF, after which fusion resumes from the corrected map. We record 15 checkpoints immediately after correction (A) and 10 after continued fusion up to the next loop closure, before its correction (B); these fusion intervals span 1.44--599.03\,s of recorded sequence and are listed in Table~\ref{tab:reply-cycles}. At each checkpoint, the Voxblox reintegration reference uses the same measurement history and corrected poses as WarpVox.

Surface accuracy remains on par with reintegration through repeated correction and fusion: mean $\Delta F@25$ is $+0.36$\,pp at A and $-0.01$\,pp at B. Continued fusion improves all four volumetric-consistency metrics in each of the ten A-to-B intervals, with mean paired gains of 2.99\,pp in support IoU, 3.52\,pp in weighted Jaccard, 0.49\,pp in free-space recall, and 1.03\,pp in sign accuracy, as new frames re-observe previously corrected regions. Table~\ref{tab:reply-cycles} reports all 25 checkpoints.

Across successive corrections, the surface-score differences relative to full reintegration do not exhibit a consistent monotonic decline. The volumetric metrics measure a different property. No ground-truth TSDF exists, so they take the reintegrated volume as the reference and form a strict spatial intersection with it, comparing support, weights, free space, and signs voxel by voxel at identical coordinates. Weighted Jaccard reaches 1 only when both volumes support the same voxels with the same weights, so any difference from the reintegrated state lowers it, whether the corrected surface scores higher or lower against the ground truth. After the first correction of 1207\_hathor, WarpVox exceeds reintegration by 2.55\,pp at F@25, yet its weighted Jaccard is 83.61\%; over all A checkpoints, a mean $\Delta F@25$ of $+0.36$\,pp coincides with a mean weighted Jaccard of 80.09\%. Because even a displacement smaller than one voxel can change voxel-wise agreement, each correction can introduce new voxel-coordinate differences. These differences can add up over successive corrections without being accompanied by a loss in geometric accuracy, while continued fusion reduces them, so weighted Jaccard falls at corrections and rises during fusion without tracking geometric accuracy. Weighted Jaccard thus measures how much support and weight still coincide with this reference voxel by voxel, not the quality of the map or of individual voxels; map quality is assessed by the GT-referenced surface scores.

To separate the fifth correction of 1207\_hathor from the correction history, we also apply it to a freshly reintegrated map with the same measurement history but no previous WarpVox corrections, which gives a weighted Jaccard of 76.93\%. Of the difference between the first and fifth corrections in the continuous run (83.61\% vs.\ 71.84\%), 6.68 points therefore arise from the fifth correction itself and 5.09 points from voxel state carried from earlier corrections, while $\Delta F@25$ at this checkpoint is $+1.12$\,pp.

Because TSDF fusion is many-to-one, a correction that updates the fused map, rather than re-fusing every stored measurement, leaves voxel-state differences in regions that are not observed again; reintegration removes them by re-fusing the full measurement history, a cost that WarpVox avoids. Across the 25 checkpoints, surface accuracy stays on par with reintegration, and continued fusion improves voxel agreement where the map is re-observed.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-S2: Conceptual distinction from ElasticFusion}{The difference seems to be the use of triangles in place of surfels for computing the deformations.}
\begin{reply}
The distinction lies not in the surface primitive but in where the correction comes from and what it returns. WarpVox corrects an externally maintained TSDF after a trajectory revision supplied by a separate SLAM back-end and returns a native TSDF for continued fusion (Sections I--II). Stored keyframe measurements are associated with the fused surface under the old poses and repositioned under the corrected poses to construct deformation targets. The mesh is an intermediate representation: reconstruction combines its corrected geometry with source support, sign, integration weights, and free space to restore the volumetric state needed for fusion.

ElasticFusion derives deformation constraints from local and global surface registration within its surfel SLAM system, so its map is corrected only by registrations that the system accepts itself. Kimera-PGMO, like WarpVox, propagates trajectory corrections, but to a timestamped mesh rather than a TSDF; like ElasticFusion, it uses an embedded deformation graph. The submitted Table III compares WarpVox with Kimera-PGMO on the same input mesh with PGMO's own optimized trajectory, isolating the correction method: WarpVox improves F@25/F@50 by 0.26/0.91 percentage points and lowers Chamfer distance from 179.0 to 152.9\,cm, with a $2.34\times$ speedup in surface correction. WarpVox then reconstructs the native TSDF in Stage 4; the complete update takes 4.792\,s, below PGMO's 8.231\,s, and the reconstructed surface exceeds PGMO at F@25/F@50. R1-3(b) demonstrates subsequent fusion and correction on WarpVox-corrected maps.
\end{reply}

\subsection*{Sec. III-A}

\point{R2-A1}{How are TSDF and weight field represented? Regular 3D grid?}
\begin{reply}
Yes. Voxblox stores distance and weight at each voxel of a regular 3-D grid, allocated sparsely in blocks. Section III-A now states this representation explicitly.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-A2}{V\textasciicircum{}- and F are not defined.}
\begin{reply}
The submitted Sec.~III-A introduces $\mathcal M^-=(V^-,F)$. The revision explicitly identifies $V^-$ as the vertex positions and $F$ as triangles stored as vertex-index triples.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-A3}{What do "active TSDF cells" mean? Cells around the zero-level set?}
\begin{reply}
These are the Marching-Cubes cells contributing triangles to $\mathcal M^-$, as introduced in the submitted Sec.~III-A. The revision repeats this definition in the cost analysis.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-A4}{What does "modality-native" mean?}
\begin{reply}
RGB-D depth images and spherical LiDAR range images, as specified after the submitted Eq.~(1). The revision replaces ``modality-native'' with these explicit names.
\end{reply}

\subsection*{Sec. III-B}

\point{R2-B1}{What kind of spatial indexing scheme allows storing/retrieving visibility relationship between vertices and sensors?}
\begin{reply}
A $k$-d tree (RGB-D) or grid (LiDAR) retrieves nearby keyframe centres under the pre-correction poses, within 14.3--14.7\,m (the 10\,m viewing frustum, depending on camera intrinsics) or 80\,m, respectively. Projection and geometric verification establish the vertex--measurement associations (Sec.~III-B); the index retrieves spatial candidates.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-B2}{What does it mean to "sample the projected depth"? Do you mean get the closest?}
\begin{reply}
For RGB-D, the depth is bilinearly interpolated from the four pixels around the projected position, with at least two valid pixels, and back-projected along the same ray. For LiDAR, the stored return closest in 3-D to the projected source vertex is selected within a $3\times3$ pixel window.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-B3}{What is the exact formula for association quality, \textbackslash{}alpha\_\{rj\}?}
\begin{reply}
The reliability is
\[
\alpha_{rj}=\frac{|\cos\phi_{rj}|}{1+z^-_{rj}/z_0}
\exp(-\delta_{rj}/\sigma_g)\exp(-|z^m_{rj}-z^-_{rj}|/\sigma_z).
\]
Here $\phi$ is the incidence angle, $\delta$ the 3-D correspondence residual, and $z^-,z^m$ the projected-vertex and measured depth/range. The last exponential applies to RGB-D only. Section III-B defines these quantities, and the parameter table gives the constants.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-B4}{What are the criteria for retaining a candidate? Can multiple candidates be retained? What if none are retained?}
\begin{reply}
A valid sample must pass the geometric gate and, for RGB-D, the reliability cutoff. Each vertex retains up to 15 candidates; with none, it follows the surface deformation without a measured target (Sec.~III-B).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-B5}{Is m\_\{rj\} a point or an image?}
\begin{reply}
$m_{rj}\in\mathbb R^3$ is the sampled sensor-frame point returned by $\mathcal S_m$ in the submitted Eq.~(2); $D_r$ is the range image.
\end{reply}

\subsection*{Sec. III-C}

\point{R2-C1}{What do RGB-D/LIDAR "records" mean? Are these just frames? Also, why are RGB-D associated with timestamp but LIDAR with poses?}
\begin{reply}
``Records'' refer to keyframe observations: RGB-D depth images or LiDAR scans. Both use the corresponding corrected pose; RGB-D retrieves it by timestamp, with interpolation when needed, and LiDAR by scan index (Sec.~III-C).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-C2}{What if there are no candidates r satisfying Eq. 5?}
\begin{reply}
For a nonempty candidate set, the median-based threshold with $\kappa=2.5$ retains at least half the candidates. If no candidate has a valid corrected pose ($\mathcal J_j=\emptyset$), the vertex is treated as unobserved: it receives no target and follows the Stage-3 deformation, so it does not pin the surface (Sec.~III-C).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-C3}{What does "supported source vertex" mean?}
\begin{reply}
An observed vertex has at least one retained observation with a valid corrected pose ($\mathcal J_j\neq\emptyset$); it yields one target $v_j^*$ and becomes a measured control. Sections III-B to III-D use ``observed vertex'' for this condition.
\end{reply}

\subsection*{Sec. III-D}

\point{R2-D1}{What do "controls" mean?}
\begin{reply}
A control is a vertex whose position is prescribed in the ARAP solve; a measured control uses the target $v_j^*$ from Stage 2 (Sec.~III-D).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D2}{What do "connected components" mean? Are these connected components of the mesh? How are they tracked?}
\begin{reply}
These are connected components of the triangle mesh: vertices connected through mesh edges belong to the same component. Components are recomputed for the current source mesh at each correction, giving labels local to that event.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D3}{What does "component-restricted inverse-distance predictor" do? I.e. what significance does connectivity have in predicting inverse distance?}
\begin{reply}
It predicts displacement from neighbouring controls using inverse-squared-distance weights. Preferring neighbours on the same mesh component separates nearby surface copies caused by drift that may require different corrections (Sec.~III-D(a), Eq.~(8)).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D4}{What is a "median - MAD residual test"? Residual between what? What is MAD?}
\begin{reply}
The test compares a measured control displacement $d_j$ with the neighbourhood prediction $\hat d_j$. Over all tested controls, define
\[
e_j=\|d_j-\hat d_j\|,\qquad
\mu=\operatorname{median}_j e_j,\qquad
\mathrm{MAD}=\operatorname{median}_j|e_j-\mu|.
\]
A control is rejected when
\[
e_j>\mu+1.5\max(1.4826\,\mathrm{MAD},0.05\,\mathrm{m}).
\]
Here MAD is the median absolute deviation; Eq.~(9), the parameter table, and Algorithm 1 specify the test, settings, and execution order.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D5}{What does "statistically isolated control" mean? Do you mean outliers?}
\begin{reply}
Yes---outlier controls. The revision uses ``outlier'' and gives the test in Eq.~(9).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D6}{What is the difference between "omitted components" (due to being "supported only by rejected controls") and "Retained components without a surviving measured control"? I.e., how can components be retained without surviving "controls" if they are omitted?}
\begin{reply}
Fragments have fewer than $N_{\rm f}=100$ faces. Control-free fragments are removed during preprocessing; fragments that lose all controls in step (a) are also omitted. The remaining components without controls receive synthetic controls in step (b).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D7}{Constraints were not yet defined here, yet there are descriptions of "receiving robust motion constraints"}
\begin{reply}
The constraint is the control displacement $d_j=v_j^*-v_j^-$, a prescribed vertex displacement; the revision defines it in Section III-D before introducing synthetic controls.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D8}{What does "QEM" stand for?}
\begin{reply}
Quadric error metric (Garland and Heckbert, 1997), expanded in Sec.~III-D.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D9}{Eq. 7: is this the "control" or the corrected proxy vertex position?}
\begin{reply}
The submitted Eq.~(7) defines the initial proxy target: the proxy rest position plus the average displacement of its clustered controls (revised Eq.~(10)).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D10}{What does "controlled proxy vertex" mean? How are vertices controlled/uncontrolled?}
\begin{reply}
A proxy vertex is controlled when it has an assigned target, from mapped controls ($\mathcal K_a\neq\emptyset$) or from the three anchors added to an otherwise unconstrained component. The remaining vertex positions are optimized in the ARAP solve (Sec.~III-D(d)--(e)).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D11}{What does "only when it is a gross residual" mean? What does it mean for residuals to be a strict majority? What are the "synthetic targets"?}
\begin{reply}
A \emph{gross residual} satisfies $r_a=\|p_a^{(0)}-\hat p_a\|\ge4.685\sigma_a$, where $\hat p_a$ is the local rigid prediction and $\sigma_a$ the robust fitting scale. A \emph{strict majority} means more than half the fitted measured targets in that component are gross. \emph{Synthetic targets} are assigned to anchor components without measured controls. Section III-D(d), Eq.~(11), specifies their update rule.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D12}{Eq. 8: p\_\{a\}\textasciicircum{}\{+\} and p\_b\textasciicircum{}\{+\} are not defined. Should these be p\_\{a\}\textasciicircum{}\{\textbackslash{}star\}? What is P\textasciicircum{}+? Why is it an optimization variable?}
\begin{reply}
$P^+=\{p_a^+\}$ are the deformed proxy positions. The targets $p_a^*$ prescribe the controlled positions; the remaining positions are optimized by ARAP. Section III-D(e) now defines both before Eq.~(12).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D13}{What does "proxy one-ring" mean?}
\begin{reply}
$\operatorname{star}_P(a)$ is the one-ring of proxy triangles incident to vertex $a$ (Sec.~III-D (e)).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D14}{Give the formula for "symmetric cotangent weights"}
\begin{reply}
$c_{bc}=\sum_{f\supset(b,c)}\tfrac12\cot\vartheta^f_{bc}$, where $\vartheta^f_{bc}$ is the angle opposite edge $(b,c)$ in incident rest triangle $f$ (Sec.~III-D(e)).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D15}{Is the proposed method using the "proxy" or the source mesh?}
\begin{reply}
We solve on the 60K QEM proxy and transfer its displacements to the source mesh. The submitted Section III-D specifies this standard configuration, and Table IV compares it with full-mesh ARAP.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D16}{What does "topology-local" mean?}
\begin{reply}
The interpolating triangle is selected from those incident to the vertex's own proxy vertex $\pi(j)$, keeping displacement transfer on the same connected surface (Sec.~III-D(f), Eq.~(13)).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D17}{What does "star(\textbackslash{}pi(j))" mean?}
\begin{reply}
$\operatorname{star}_P(\pi(j))$ is the set of proxy triangles incident to $\pi(j)$ (Sec.~III-D (f), Eq.~(13)).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D18}{How are "edge-stretch, area-change or aspect-ratio bounds" defined mathematically?}
\begin{reply}
Equation (14) defines
\[
\xi^{\rm e}=\max_e\frac{\ell_e^+}{\ell_e^-},\qquad
\xi^{\rm A}=\frac{A^+}{A^-},\qquad
\xi^{\rm asp}=\frac{(\ell^+_{\max})^2}{2A^+},
\]
where $\ell$ and $A$ are edge length and face area, and $-/+$ denote source/warped geometry. A face is flagged at $\xi^{\rm e}\ge3$, $\xi^{\rm A}\ge6$, or $\xi^{\rm asp}\ge20$ with $\ell^+_{\max}\ge0.5$\,m. These bounds are listed in the parameter table.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D19}{What does "observed support" mean?}
\begin{reply}
Observed source support is $\Omega^-=\{x:w^-(x)>0\}$. It contains voxels carrying integration evidence, including both the surface neighbourhood and observed free space. Section III-A separates this volumetric set from observed mesh vertices, which are defined by retained keyframe correspondences in Section III-B.
\end{reply}

\subsection*{Sec. III-E}

\point{R2-E1}{What is "surface-local reconstruction/domain"? Is there a "surface-global reconstruction/domain"?}
\begin{reply}
Surface-local reconstruction processes voxel centres within truncation distance $\tau$ of the warped surface, restricted to blocks containing a warped vertex and their 26 neighbours. The second part of Stage 4 transports supported source free space.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E2}{The first paragraph says the "sign orientation" comes from the source mesh, whereas Eq. 12 claims it is "determined separately from the source field".}
\begin{reply}
The source TSDF provides the sign orientation: the mesh normal defines geometric orientation, and valid source-field samples establish which side is positive free space. The revised Stage-4 opening and Eq.~(19) state this rule and its fallbacks explicitly.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E3}{What does "weight provides support" mean?}
\begin{reply}
Positive TSDF weight denotes observed support. Transferring this weight retains both that support and its integration strength for subsequent fusion (Sec.~III-E, Eq.~(18)).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E4}{What is the fallback branch?}
\begin{reply}
The fallback initializes voxels within $\rho=1.5s$ of the warped surface that have no transferred source weight. It uses the warped geometry and a prior weight in $[0,1]$, excluding open boundaries (Sec.~III-E, Eq.~(20)).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E5}{What is a "consistent source sample"?}
\begin{reply}
A trilinear source sample is valid when every voxel with a nonzero interpolation coefficient has positive integration weight. It is sign-consistent when those voxels also share the same nonzero distance sign. Section III-E defines the two tests before Eq.~(18).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E6}{What is the difference between w\_\{tr\}\textasciicircum{}+ and w\_\{src\}\textasciicircum{}+?}
\begin{reply}
The earlier $w_{\rm tr}^+$ duplicated $w_{\rm src}^+$ on the transferred-voxel set. We removed the redundant symbol and the former Eq.~(13). Equation (18) defines the source weight once, and Eq.~(22) uses it directly on $\Omega_{\rm tr}$.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E7}{How is n\_f\textasciicircum{}+ defined?}
\begin{reply}
$n_f^+$ is the unit normal of warped face $f=(a,b,c)$, oriented by the vertex order of $F$; Sec.~III-E also defines the paired frames built from it (Eq.~(16)).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E8}{How does the algorithm determine if the closest surface feature is an open boundary?}
\begin{reply}
Boundary edges are incident to exactly one face. The prior is withheld when the closest point lies on such an edge or its end vertex, keeping geometric fallback support away from open boundaries (Sec.~III-E, fallback paragraph).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E9}{What does "supported voxel" mean?}
\begin{reply}
A supported voxel has positive weight, $w>0$ (Sec.~III-A); $\Omega^+$ is the set of supported output voxels (Eq.~(22)).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E10}{What is a "fallback support"? What are "narrow samples"?}
\begin{reply}
\emph{Fallback support} is the set of voxels with positive fallback weight, $\Omega_{\rm fb}=\{x:w_{\rm fb}(x)>0\}$. \emph{Narrow samples} are transferred voxels within distance $\rho$ of the warped surface, denoted $\mathcal N_{\rm tr}$ in the revision (Sec.~III-E).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E11}{What makes a source face valid or invalid?}
\begin{reply}
Each transport cell takes the source face closest to the centroid of its observed voxels; there is no distance limit. The face is valid if neither it nor its warped copy is degenerate, so that the paired frames of Eq.~(21) can be constructed; a cell whose closest face is invalid is not transported (Sec.~III-E).
\end{reply}

\subsection*{Sec. III-F}

\point{R2-F1}{What are C\_\{def\} and C\_\{vol\}?}
\begin{reply}
$C_{\rm def}$ is the Stage-3 cost of deformation, including proxy construction, target consolidation, ARAP, and transfer or repair. $C_{\rm vol}$ is the Stage-4 cost of TSDF reconstruction and free-space transport (Sec.~III-F).
\end{reply}

\subsection*{Sec. IV-A}

\point{R2-IVA1}{Fully define F@10/25/50. Are these F1-score at 10/25/50cm thresholds?}
\begin{reply}
Yes. The submitted Section IV-A specifies bidirectional nearest-neighbour tests at 10/25/50\,cm. Explicitly, $F=100\cdot2PR/(P+R)$, where $P$ and $R$ are the fractions of predicted and reference surface points, respectively, within the threshold of the other surface.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-IVA2}{Please also add Chamfer distance, a standard metric for geometric similarity.}
\begin{reply}
We computed the symmetric, unsquared Chamfer distance for all reconstruction baselines and the PGMO comparison:
\[
\mathrm{CD}=\frac12\left(\frac{1}{|\mathcal P|}\sum_{p\in\mathcal P}d(p,\mathcal G)
+\frac{1}{|\mathcal G|}\sum_{g\in\mathcal G}d(g,\mathcal P)\right).
\]
Here $\mathcal P$ and $\mathcal G$ are the predicted and reference point sets, and $d(p,\mathcal G)$ is the nearest-neighbour distance. The table averages per-event, untruncated distances in centimetres over the same cases as the F-scores.
\begin{center}\small

On the ten-sequence corrected-surface comparison (Table III), CD is 309.6\,cm for the input mesh, 179.0\,cm for Kimera-PGMO one-shot correction, the stronger of its two modes (183.8\,cm online), and 152.9\,cm for WarpVox. WarpVox reduces CD by 26.1\,cm relative to one-shot PGMO under shared inputs.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-IVA3}{Table II shows inconsistent relative portions between different datasets. Please discuss why some datasets incur larger time in, e.g., association, whereas other datasets incur larger time in target estimation.}
\begin{reply}
Stage times depend on different workloads: association processes candidate vertex--keyframe pairs, target construction processes retained observations, and deformation/reconstruction depend on surface and volume size. RGB-D queries every source vertex, whereas LiDAR queries one per occupied 0.5\,m cell and searches spherical-image neighbourhoods. These modality and map-size differences affect the stage proportions.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-IVA4}{Table III: What are "ten bags"?}
\begin{reply}
A ``bag'' is the ROS recording of one robot's Kimera-Multi sequence. Table III compares the final states of the ten sequences identified in Table~\ref{tab:reply-r1a-paired}. The revised caption uses ``ten Kimera-Multi sequences.''
\end{reply}
