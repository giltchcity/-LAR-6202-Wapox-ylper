\point{R1-2: Stage-3 pseudocode}{The four-stage structure is easy to follow, and the introduction clearly motivates the problem. Section III-D is harder to follow because several filtering, target-adjustment, and mesh-repair operations are described in succession. Pseudocode showing their order would help.}
\begin{reply}
Algorithm 1 gives the execution order and dependencies of Stage-3 filtering, target consolidation, deformation, and mesh repair. Each step refers to its definition in Section III-D. A repair pass stops when it reduces neither the number of invalid faces nor the number of aspect-ratio violations, with at most three passes and 80 Jacobi steps per pass.

The execution sequence is reproduced below. The input is the source triangle mesh and its measured control displacements; the output is a source/warped surface pair with shared face indices.
\begin{enumerate}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
\item Label mesh components. Remove degenerate faces and components with fewer than 100 faces that contain no measured control.
\item Predict each measured control displacement from neighbouring controls. Apply the median--MAD rejection once. Remove small components that lose all controls; give every other unconstrained component three synthetic controls.
\item For more than 78,000 source vertices, build the component-aware QEM proxy, average the control displacements within each proxy cluster, and consolidate the proxy targets using the Welsch rule. Use the direct source-mesh path when the proxy fails the checks listed in R2-D15.
\item Solve ARAP for 20 local--global iterations with prescribed control positions. On the proxy path, transfer the resulting displacements to source vertices through incident proxy triangles.
\item Test edge stretch, area growth, and aspect ratio. Relax the displacements of vertices on flagged faces, re-test, and stop when the faces satisfy the criteria or the repair makes no progress, subject to the iteration limits above.
\item Remove faces that still violate the edge or area bound from both surfaces. Preserve the shared face indices for Stage 4.
\end{enumerate}
\end{reply}

\textbf{Reconstructing observation support and displacement constraints.}
TSDF fusion combines posed range observations from multiple frames into a shared distance field through weighted integration (\href{https://arxiv.org/html/1611.03631#S4}{Voxblox, Sec.~IV}). WarpVox follows this construction in reverse at the level of observation support: starting from the fused surface, Stage 1 projects each source vertex into candidate keyframes under the pre-correction poses and retains geometrically consistent measurements. Stage 2 then repositions these measurements under the corrected poses and aggregates them into surface targets. The old poses establish which observations support the existing geometry; the corrected poses determine where those observations move.

The measured-sample construction retains each observation's sensor-frame geometry. For the retained set $I_j$ with nonnegative weights and positive total weight, the target is the unique weighted least-squares solution:
\[
\begin{aligned}
v_j^*&=\operatorname*{argmin}_{x\in\mathbb R^3} E_j(x)
=\frac{\sum_{r\in I_j}\omega_{rj}q_{rj}^{\mathrm{meas}}}{\sum_{r\in I_j}\omega_{rj}},\\
E_j(x)&=\sum_{r\in I_j}\omega_{rj}\|x-q_{rj}^{\mathrm{meas}}\|^2.
\end{aligned}
\]
Indeed, $E_j(x)-E_j(v_j^*)=(\sum_{r\in I_j}\omega_{rj})\|x-v_j^*\|^2$.
In a local plane-distance model with common unit normal $n$, the weighted field of these samples is $D_j(x)=n^\top(x-v_j^*)$, so $D_j(v_j^*)=0$. This connects the target to the fused zero plane of the retained observations in that model. Section III-C now states this construction and its relation to distance fusion.

The matched comparison retains this observation support and tests two ways of constructing candidate target coordinates in Stage 2. Writing the source vertex in the pre-correction keyframe coordinates as $\hat v_{rj}^-$, the two candidates are
\[
\begin{aligned}
q_{rj}^{\mathrm{meas}} &= \widehat R_r^+ m_{rj}+\widehat t_r^+,\\
q_{rj}^{\mathrm{pose}} &= \widehat R_r^+\hat v_{rj}^-+\widehat t_r^+,\\
q_{rj}^{\mathrm{meas}}-q_{rj}^{\mathrm{pose}}
&=\widehat R_r^+(m_{rj}-\hat v_{rj}^-).
\end{aligned}
\]
Here $m_{rj}$ is the associated sensor-frame measurement and $(\widehat R_r^+,\widehat t_r^+)$ is its corrected keyframe pose. Stage 1 bounds $\|m_{rj}-\hat v_{rj}^-\|$ through its association gate. Both constructions apply the same keyframe pose corrections through the reconstructed observation support; their candidate coordinates differ by the rotated association residual. The close F@25 and F@50 scores are consistent with this bounded difference.

\textbf{Direct ablation of observation association.}

This experiment directly evaluates the geometric verification that establishes the observation support shared by both Stage-2 constructions.

WarpVox reconstructs the observation support of an already fused surface and uses it to convert back-end pose corrections into deformation constraints. Source-supported reconstruction combines the corrected surface with the volume's observed support, integration weights, sign information, and free space to produce a native TSDF for continued fusion. \end{reply}

\point{R2-S2: Conceptual distinction from ElasticFusion}{The difference seems to be the use of triangles in place of surfels for computing the deformations.}
\begin{reply}
WarpVox corrects an externally maintained TSDF after a back-end trajectory revision. Fusion combines multiple observations into a shared volume. Stage 1 reconstructs the observation support of its surface through verified keyframe associations; Stage 2 repositions and aggregates the associated measurements under the corrected poses to construct deformation targets. The triangle mesh carries this surface deformation between the input and output TSDFs. Its reconstruction combines distances from the warped surface with source-derived support, sign, integration weights, and free space, preserving the volumetric state needed to resume TSDF fusion. ElasticFusion and Kimera-PGMO both use embedded deformation graphs: ElasticFusion derives constraints from local and global surface registration within its surfel SLAM system, while Kimera-PGMO propagates trajectory corrections to a timestamped mesh. The contributions are observation-guided deformation constraints and source-supported volumetric reconstruction; ARAP and QEM provide the deformation machinery. The submitted Table III already compares surface correction under the same input mesh and pre-/post-correction trajectories: WarpVox improves F@25/F@50 by 0.26/0.91 percentage points over Kimera-PGMO with a $2.34\times$ speedup in surface correction. Stage 4 then reconstructs a native TSDF in another 1.277\,s, for 4.792\,s in total. The repeated-cycle experiment demonstrates that the resulting volume supports subsequent fusion and correction.
\end{reply}

\end{reply}

\end{reply}

\point{R2-A4}{What does "modality-native" mean?}

\subsection*{Sec. III-B}

\point{R2-B1}{What kind of spatial indexing scheme allows storing/retrieving visibility relationship between vertices and sensors?}
\begin{reply}
Candidate keyframes are retrieved by spatial proximity of their sensor centres under the pre-correction poses: a $k$-d tree with a 14.7\,m radius for RGB-D, and a grid with an 80\,m search radius for LiDAR. The index is rebuilt at each correction. Projecting a vertex into each candidate range image and applying the gate in Eq.~(2) establishes measurement consistency. In particular, an occluder's return is rejected when its discrepancy from the vertex exceeds the gate tolerance.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-B2}{What does it mean to "sample the projected depth"? Do you mean get the closest?}
\begin{reply}
For RGB-D, the depth is bilinearly interpolated from the four pixels around the projected position, with at least two valid pixels, and back-projected along the same ray. For LiDAR, the stored return closest in 3-D to the projected source vertex is selected within a $3\times3$ pixel window.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-B3}{What is the exact formula for association quality, \textbackslash{}alpha\_\{rj\}?}
\begin{reply}
Section III-B now defines the association reliability as
\[
\alpha_{rj}=\frac{|\cos\phi_{rj}|}{1+z^-_{rj}/z_0}
\exp(-\delta_{rj}/\sigma_g)\exp(-|z^m_{rj}-z^-_{rj}|/\sigma_z).
\]
Here $\delta_{rj}=\|m_{rj}-\hat v_{rj}^-\|$ is the distance between the sampled point and the projected source vertex in the sensor frame. The quantities $z^-_{rj}$ and $z^m_{rj}$ are their depths (RGB-D) or ranges (LiDAR), and $\phi_{rj}$ is the angle between the source normal and the viewing ray. The factors favour frontal, nearby observations that agree with the source geometry; the last exponential applies to RGB-D only. The gate is $\delta_{rj}\leq g_0+g_1z^-_{rj}$; LiDAR also requires $|z^m_{rj}-z^-_{rj}|$ to meet the same bound. R1-3(c) lists the modality-specific constants and the subsequent target weights $\omega_{rj}$.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-B4}{What are the criteria for retaining a candidate? Can multiple candidates be retained? What if none are retained?}
\begin{reply}
A candidate is retained when a valid sample passes the geometric gate and, for RGB-D, satisfies $\alpha_{rj}\ge\alpha_{\min}$. Up to $n_{\max}=15$ of the most reliable candidates are retained per vertex. LiDAR collects at most 30 candidates in grid-cell and then trajectory order before selecting the retained set. A vertex with no retained observation follows the Stage-3 deformation without a measured target (Sec.~III-B).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-B5}{Is m\_\{rj\} a point or an image?}
\begin{reply}
$m_{rj}\in\mathbb R^3$ is the sampled sensor-frame point returned by $\mathcal S_m$ in the submitted Eq.~(2); $D_r$ is the range image.
\end{reply}

\subsection*{Sec. III-C}

\point{R2-C1}{What do RGB-D/LIDAR "records" mean? Are these just frames? Also, why are RGB-D associated with timestamp but LIDAR with poses?}
\begin{reply}
``Records'' are observations, each from one keyframe (an RGB-D depth image or a LiDAR scan). Both modalities use the corrected pose of that keyframe: a LiDAR scan has its own trajectory entry, while an RGB-D keyframe's corrected pose is read from $T^+$ at its timestamp or interpolated between the bracketing poses when they are at most three median pose intervals apart (Sec.~III-C, Table~I).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-C2}{What if there are no candidates r satisfying Eq. 5?}
\begin{reply}
For a nonempty set of observations with valid corrected poses, the trimming threshold is at least the median residual because $\kappa>1$. At least half of those observations survive; with fewer than three, all are retained. If no observation has a valid corrected pose, $\mathcal J_j=\emptyset$ and $v_j^*=v_j^-$, yielding a zero-displacement measured control. A vertex with no retained observation in Stage 1 follows the surface deformation without a measured target. Sections III-B and III-C distinguish these two cases.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-C3}{What does "supported source vertex" mean?}
\begin{reply}
An observed vertex has at least one retained observation ($\mathcal O_j\neq\emptyset$); it yields one target $v_j^*$ and becomes a measured control. Sections III-B to III-D use ``observed vertex'' for this condition.
\end{reply}

\subsection*{Sec. III-D}

\point{R2-D1}{What do "controls" mean?}
\begin{reply}
A control is a vertex with a prescribed deformed position. Each observed source vertex becomes a measured control with target $v_j^*$ and displacement $d_j=v_j^*-v_j^-$. Components without a surviving measured control receive the synthetic controls described in R2-D7. The ARAP solve fixes controlled positions and solves for the remaining vertex positions; subsequent repair is described separately in R2-D18.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D2}{What do "connected components" mean? Are these connected components of the mesh? How are they tracked?}
\begin{reply}
These are connected components of the triangle mesh: vertices connected through mesh edges belong to the same component. Components are recomputed for the current source mesh at each correction, giving labels local to that event.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D3}{What does "component-restricted inverse-distance predictor" do? I.e. what significance does connectivity have in predicting inverse distance?}
\begin{reply}
The predictor estimates a control displacement as the inverse-squared-distance weighted mean of neighbouring control displacements (Eq.~(8)). Among the $k=20$ nearest controls, it uses those on the same connected component when at least $n_{\min}=16$ are available; otherwise it uses all $k$. Component identity helps distinguish nearby copies of a surface produced by drift, which can require different corrections despite their spatial proximity.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D4}{What is a "median - MAD residual test"? Residual between what? What is MAD?}
\begin{reply}
The test compares a measured control displacement $d_j$ with the neighbourhood prediction $\hat d_j$. Over all tested controls, define
\[
e_j=\|d_j-\hat d_j\|,\qquad
\mu=\operatorname{median}_j e_j,\qquad
\mathrm{MAD}=\operatorname{median}_j|e_j-\mu|.
\]
A control is rejected when
\[
e_j>\mu+1.5\max(1.4826\,\mathrm{MAD},0.05\,\mathrm{m}).
\]
The factor 1.4826 converts MAD into a Gaussian-consistent scale estimate; the 0.05\,m floor prevents a nearly constant neighbourhood from producing a vanishing threshold. This rejection is applied once, before synthetic controls and proxy construction, as specified in Eq.~(9) and Algorithm 1.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D5}{What does "statistically isolated control" mean? Do you mean outliers?}
\begin{reply}
Yes---outlier controls. The revision uses ``outlier'' and gives the test in Eq.~(9).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D6}{What is the difference between "omitted components" (due to being "supported only by rejected controls") and "Retained components without a surviving measured control"? I.e., how can components be retained without surviving "controls" if they are omitted?}
\begin{reply}
Omitted components are fragments (fewer than $N_{\rm f}$ faces) that initially carried controls and lost all of them in step (a). Every other component without a surviving control, including one that never had a control, is retained and receives synthetic controls (step (b)).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D7}{Constraints were not yet defined here, yet there are descriptions of "receiving robust motion constraints"}
\begin{reply}
The submitted Sec.~III-D introduces the control displacements $d_j=v_j^*-v_j^-$; the revision states their role as prescribed vertex positions before describing synthetic constraints. For each retained component without a surviving measured control, step (b) places synthetic controls at the lowest-index vertex, the vertex farthest from it, and the vertex farthest from both. Their common displacement is the coordinate-wise median displacement of the 20 surviving controls nearest to the component centroid. These prescribed positions supply the rigid motion that the component's ARAP energy leaves undetermined.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D8}{What does "QEM" stand for?}
\begin{reply}
Quadric error metric (Garland and Heckbert, 1997), expanded in Sec.~III-D.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D9}{Eq. 7: is this the "control" or the corrected proxy vertex position?}
\begin{reply}
The submitted Eq.~(7) defines the initial proxy target $p_a^{(0)}$: the proxy rest position plus the average displacement of its clustered controls. The Welsch consolidation produces the constrained target $p_a^*$, and the ARAP solve produces the deformed position $p_a^+$. These three quantities are distinguished in Eqs.~(10)--(12).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D10}{What does "controlled proxy vertex" mean? How are vertices controlled/uncontrolled?}
\begin{reply}
A proxy vertex is controlled if at least one control maps to it ($\mathcal K_a\neq\emptyset$); a proxy component that receives no control, e.g., one split off when degenerate proxy triangles are dropped, is held at three of its vertices, which step (d) revises like synthetic targets. Only uncontrolled vertices are unknowns of Eq.~(12).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D11}{What does "only when it is a gross residual" mean? What does it mean for residuals to be a strict majority? What are the "synthetic targets"?}
\begin{reply}
For controlled proxy vertex $a$, a local rigid fit predicts $\hat p_a=\hat R_ap_a^-+\hat t_a$ from neighbouring measured proxy targets. The residual is $r_a=\|p_a^{(0)}-\hat p_a\|$. A measured target is classified as gross when $r_a\ge4.685\sigma_a$, where $\sigma_a$ is the median fitting residual plus 1.4826 times its MAD.

Let $N_K$ be the number of fitted measured targets in component $K$, and $G_K$ the number classified as gross. A strict majority means $2G_K>N_K$. Equation (11) then gives
\[
p_a^*=\begin{cases}
\hat p_a, & a\text{ is gross and }2G_K>N_K,\\
\hat p_a+\nu(r_a)(p_a^{(0)}-\hat p_a), & a\text{ has a synthetic target},\\
p_a^{(0)}, & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\qquad \nu(r)=\exp[-r^2/(2\sigma_a^2)].
\]
The fit uses up to 20 nearest measured proxy controls, excluding $a$, with at least six required for fitting. It performs four rounds of Welsch reweighting multiplied by a fixed prior favouring near, distance-preserving, and well-observed neighbours. At fixed scale, the influence $r\nu(r)$ tends to zero for large residuals, giving geometrically consistent neighbours greater influence on the rigid fit. A target with too few fitting neighbours keeps its initial value.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D12}{Eq. 8: p\_\{a\}\textasciicircum{}\{+\} and p\_b\textasciicircum{}\{+\} are not defined. Should these be p\_\{a\}\textasciicircum{}\{\textbackslash{}star\}? What is P\textasciicircum{}+? Why is it an optimization variable?}
\begin{reply}
$P^+=\{p_a^+\}$ are the deformed proxy positions: controlled ones are fixed to $p_a^*$, and the others are the unknowns of Eq.~(12), now written in the spokes-and-rims form that libigl solves (step (e)). ARAP propagates the prescribed corrections while penalizing local shape distortion: under a common rigid motion $p_a^+=Rp_a^-+t$, choosing every local rotation as $R$ makes each edge residual zero.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D13}{What does "proxy one-ring" mean?}
\begin{reply}
$\operatorname{star}_P(a)$ is the one-ring of proxy triangles incident to vertex $a$ (Sec.~III-D (e)).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D14}{Give the formula for "symmetric cotangent weights"}
\begin{reply}
$c^f_{bc}=\frac12\cot\vartheta^f_{bc}$, with $\vartheta^f_{bc}$ the angle opposite edge $(b,c)$ in rest triangle $f$; summed over the two triangles of an edge, this is the symmetric cotangent weight (Sec.~III-D (e)). The factor $1/3$ in Eq.~(12) offsets that each triangle is visited from its three corners.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D15}{Is the proposed method using the "proxy" or the source mesh?}
\begin{reply}
Meshes with more than $1.3P_{\max}$ vertices use a coarse proxy for the ARAP solve, with $P_{\max}=60{,}000$. This reduces the number of deformation unknowns; QEM places proxy vertices according to squared distances to the source face planes. The proxy displacements are then added to the original source vertices, which supply the output surface. Smaller meshes are solved directly. The implementation also falls back to the source mesh when proxy construction yields fewer than 100 vertices or faces, fewer than 10 controlled vertices, a controlled-vertex fraction above 99\%, or failed ARAP precomputation.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D16}{What does "topology-local" mean?}
\begin{reply}
The interpolating triangle is selected from the proxy triangles incident to the vertex's own proxy vertex $\pi(j)$, keeping motion transfer on the same connected surface. Its nonnegative, unit-sum barycentric weights interpolate the displacements and reproduce constant translations exactly (Sec.~III-D (f), Eq.~(13)).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D17}{What does "star(\textbackslash{}pi(j))" mean?}
\begin{reply}
$\operatorname{star}_P(\pi(j))$ is the set of proxy triangles incident to $\pi(j)$ (Sec.~III-D (f), Eq.~(13)).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D18}{How are "edge-stretch, area-change or aspect-ratio bounds" defined mathematically?}
\begin{reply}
For corresponding source and warped triangle edge lengths $\ell_e^-$ and $\ell_e^+$, areas $A^-$ and $A^+$, and longest warped edge $\ell^+_{\max}$, the measures are
\[
\xi^{\rm e}=\max_e\frac{\ell_e^+}{\ell_e^-},\qquad
\xi^{\rm A}=\frac{A^+}{A^-},\qquad
\xi^{\rm asp}=\frac{(\ell^+_{\max})^2}{2A^+}.
\]
A face is flagged when $\xi^{\rm e}\ge3$, $\xi^{\rm A}\ge6$, or both $\xi^{\rm asp}\ge20$ and $\ell^+_{\max}\ge0.5$\,m. Jacobi steps relax the displacements of its vertices, including controlled vertices, toward their neighbour means while holding other vertices fixed. Faces are re-tested after each pass. Repair uses at most three passes with at most 80 steps each and stops when neither the invalid-face count nor the aspect-violation count decreases. Faces still violating the edge or area bound are removed from both paired surfaces.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-D19}{What does "observed support" mean?}
\begin{reply}
Observed source support is $\Omega^-=\{x:w^-(x)>0\}$. It contains voxels carrying integration evidence, including both the surface neighbourhood and observed free space. Section III-A separates this volumetric set from observed mesh vertices, which are defined by retained keyframe correspondences in Section III-B.
\end{reply}

\subsection*{Sec. III-E}

\point{R2-E1}{What is "surface-local reconstruction/domain"? Is there a "surface-global reconstruction/domain"?}
\begin{reply}
Surface-local reconstruction processes voxel centres within truncation distance $\tau$ of the warped surface, restricted to blocks containing a warped vertex and their 26 neighbours. The second part of Stage 4 transports supported source free space.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E2}{The first paragraph says the "sign orientation" comes from the source mesh, whereas Eq. 12 claims it is "determined separately from the source field".}
\begin{reply}
The warped surface supplies distance magnitudes and geometric normals. The source TSDF guides the transferred sign through the central difference across the paired source face; when that quantity is unavailable, the rule uses a sign-consistent interpolated source sample, then the warped-face sign as a final fallback. The revised Stage-4 opening and Eq.~(19) specify these sources and their priority.

Specifically, for corresponding closest points $q^-$ and $q^+$ on the source and warped faces, define
\[
g_f=\frac{\bar\Phi^-(q^-+s n_f^-)-\bar\Phi^-(q^--s n_f^-)}{2s},
\qquad s_f^+(x)=\operatorname{sgn}((x-q^+)^\top n_f^+).
\]
The source-field orientation is available when the source sample at the pulled-back voxel and both probes are valid and $g_f\ne0$. The transferred sign is then $s_f^+(x)\operatorname{sgn}(g_f)$. Otherwise, the sign-consistent interpolated source value is used when available; the final fallback is $s_f^+(x)$. Thus, the mesh supplies the oriented geometric frame, while the source field checks its free-space orientation.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E3}{What does "weight provides support" mean?}
\begin{reply}
A positive source weight records observed support and sets the influence of subsequent measurements. Before truncation, integrating a new signed-distance measurement $d$ with weight $\omega$ gives
\[
\Phi_{\rm next}=\Phi^+ + \frac{\omega}{w^++\omega}(d-\Phi^+).
\]
Thus, $w^+$ determines how strongly new evidence changes the corrected field. When the trilinear source sample is valid, Eq.~(18) transfers its interpolated weight. Otherwise, it takes the weight of the containing source voxel. In both cases, the corrected distance magnitude is recomputed from the warped surface. The corrected voxel therefore combines the new geometry with source-derived integration strength.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E4}{What is the fallback branch?}
\begin{reply}
The fallback handles voxels with no transferred source weight within $\rho=1.5s$ of the warped surface. With $r(x)$ the distance to that surface, the weight is
\[
w_{\rm fb}(x)=\min\{1,\max\{0,(\rho-r(x))/s\}\}.
\]
The distance magnitude comes from the warped mesh and its sign from the oriented warped face. The weight, bounded by one and decreasing away from the surface, initializes geometric support that subsequent observations can refine through the fusion rule in R2-E3. The prior is withheld at open boundaries, as detailed in R2-E8. Equation (20) and the fallback paragraph state these conditions explicitly.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E5}{What is a "consistent source sample"?}
\begin{reply}
A trilinear source sample is valid when every voxel with a nonzero interpolation coefficient has positive integration weight. It is sign-consistent when those voxels also share the same nonzero distance sign. Section III-E defines the two tests before Eq.~(18).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E6}{What is the difference between w\_\{tr\}\textasciicircum{}+ and w\_\{src\}\textasciicircum{}+?}
\begin{reply}
The earlier $w_{\rm tr}^+$ duplicated $w_{\rm src}^+$ on the transferred-voxel set. We removed the redundant symbol and the former Eq.~(13). Equation (18) defines the source weight once, and Eq.~(22) uses it directly on $\Omega_{\rm tr}$.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E7}{How is n\_f\textasciicircum{}+ defined?}
\begin{reply}
$n_f^+$ is the unit normal of warped face $f=(a,b,c)$, oriented by the vertex order of $F$; Sec.~III-E also defines the paired frames built from it (Eq.~(16)).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E8}{How does the algorithm determine if the closest surface feature is an open boundary?}
\begin{reply}
Boundary edges are incident to exactly one face. The prior is withheld when the closest point lies on such an edge or its end vertex, keeping geometric fallback support away from open boundaries (Sec.~III-E, fallback paragraph).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E9}{What does "supported voxel" mean?}
\begin{reply}
A supported voxel has positive weight, $w>0$ (Sec.~III-A); $\Omega^+$ is the set of supported output voxels (Eq.~(22)).
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E10}{What is a "fallback support"? What are "narrow samples"?}
\begin{reply}
Fallback support is $\Omega_{\rm fb}=\{x:w_{\rm fb}(x)>0\}$. The former phrase ``narrow samples'' refers to the narrow set $\mathcal N_{\rm tr}$ of voxels with positive transferred source weight and distance $r(x)\le\rho$ from the warped surface. The extended set $\mathcal E$ covers $\rho<r(x)\le\tau$: a voxel with a valid trilinear source sample is retained there only when the source-field central difference $g_f$ fixes its sign; a weight-only voxel uses the warped-face sign.

After removing sign conflicts, a face-connected flood fill from $\mathcal N_{\rm tr}\cup\Omega_{\rm fb}$ removes disconnected extended voxels. The retained narrow and extended voxels form $\Omega_{\rm tr}$. Transported free space is inserted last into zero-weight output voxels, with insertions in the 26-neighbourhood of retained negative voxels rejected. Any two corners of a Marching-Cubes cell are 26-neighbours, so these positive insertions cannot enable a new sign-crossing cell. Free-space transport retains observed free regions beyond the reconstructed surface band. Figure~\ref{fig:reply-mechanisms}(c) and Section III-E distinguish these sets and their roles.
\end{reply}

\point{R2-E11}{What makes a source face valid or invalid?}
\begin{reply}
For each free-space group, the algorithm selects the closest source face within $16s$ of the group's mean voxel centre. The transfer is applied when a face is found and both the source face and its warped copy are nondegenerate, allowing the paired orthonormal frames $R^-,R^+$ to be constructed. For corresponding face points $q^-,q^+$, the forward map is
\[
\Gamma(y)=q^++R^+(R^-)^\top(y-q^-),\qquad
\|\Gamma(y)-q^+\|=\|y-q^-\|.
\]
It preserves each source voxel's offset magnitude relative to the paired face. Under a common rigid correction $(R,t)$, $q^+=Rq^-+t$ and $R^+=RR^-$, so $\Gamma(y)=Ry+t$ exactly. The surface-local pullback in Eq.~(16) uses the inverse frame rotation. Groups without valid paired faces are discarded (Sec.~III-E, Eq.~(21)).
\end{reply}

\subsection*{Sec. III-F}

\point{R2-F1}{What are C\_\{def\} and C\_\{vol\}?}
\begin{reply}
$C_{\rm def}$ is the Stage-3 cost of deformation, including proxy construction, target consolidation, ARAP, and transfer or repair. $C_{\rm vol}$ is the Stage-4 cost of TSDF reconstruction and free-space transport (Sec.~III-F).
\end{reply}

\subsection*{Sec. IV-A}

\point{R2-IVA1}{Fully define F@10/25/50. Are these F1-score at 10/25/50cm thresholds?}
\begin{reply}
Yes. The submitted Section IV-A specifies F1-score thresholds of 10, 25, and 50\,cm. The revision adds the explicit precision and recall definitions. Let $\mathcal P$ and $\mathcal G$ be the predicted and reference point sets and $d(p,\mathcal X)=\min_{x\in\mathcal X}\|p-x\|$. We use
\[
P_\eta=\frac{|\{p\in\mathcal P:d(p,\mathcal G)<\eta\}|}{|\mathcal P|},\quad
R_\eta=\frac{|\{g\in\mathcal G:d(g,\mathcal P)<\eta\}|}{|\mathcal G|},\quad
F_\eta=100\frac{2P_\eta R_\eta}{P_\eta+R_\eta}.
\]
For a nonempty pair of point sets with $P_\eta+R_\eta=0$, the score is zero. Predicted meshes are sampled uniformly by area with two million points; the reference is the full cropped ground-truth cloud. Wavemap uses its occupancy point cloud, randomly subsampled to at most two million points. This definition, sampling protocol, and percentage convention form the replacement Metrics paragraph in Section IV-A.

\point{R2-IVA2}{Please also add Chamfer distance, a standard metric for geometric similarity.}
\begin{reply}
We computed the symmetric, unsquared Chamfer distance for all reconstruction baselines and the PGMO comparison:
\[
\mathrm{CD}=\frac12\left(\frac{1}{|\mathcal P|}\sum_{p\in\mathcal P}d(p,\mathcal G)
+\frac{1}{|\mathcal G|}\sum_{g\in\mathcal G}d(g,\mathcal P)\right).
\]
The table reports the arithmetic mean of the per-event, untruncated distances in centimetres. The two directed means have equal weight, independently of the numbers of predicted and reference points. Distances are evaluated on the same cases as the F-scores.

\end{reply}

\point{R2-IVA3}{Table II shows inconsistent relative portions between different datasets. Please discuss why some datasets incur larger time in, e.g., association, whereas other datasets incur larger time in target estimation.}
\begin{reply}
The relative stage times reflect modality-specific association work and map size. RGB-D queries every source vertex and transforms up to 15 retained measurements per vertex, with corrected poses interpolated by timestamp where needed. LiDAR queries one vertex per occupied 0.5\,m cell and reads corrected scan poses directly. Its association also searches a $3\times3$ spherical-image window. These differences help explain the RGB-D association/target times of 0.199/0.399\,s and the KITTI values of 0.310/0.069\,s. Deformation and TSDF reconstruction depend on surface and volume size; their KITTI times are 2.457 and 1.888\,s, compared with 1.419 and 0.231\,s on Kimera-Multi at 25\,cm. The 10\,cm Kimera row averages a separate 11-case subset. The timing columns correspond to Stage 1 (Assoc.), Stage 2 (Target), Stage 3 (ARAP and Topol.), and Stage 4 (TSDF).
\end{reply}

\end{reply}
